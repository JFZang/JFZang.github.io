Firm Size and Stock Volatility Dynamics

An empirical study of whether market capitalisation is associated with the way individual stocks respond to volatility shocks and how long those shocks persist.

40 NYSE stocks Monthly data: Dec 2002–Feb 2023 By Jiefu Zhang

The result in one view

The stock-level models show clear volatility clustering and persistence. However, ordered trend tests do not support a statistically significant monotonic relationship between firm size and the estimated GARCH parameters.

Research question

Does company size shape volatility dynamics?

Smaller companies are often described as riskier than larger companies, but higher average volatility does not necessarily imply that their volatility responds differently to new information or remains elevated for longer. This project separates those ideas by asking whether the parameters governing conditional volatility vary systematically with market capitalisation.

This is a firm-characteristics study rather than a direct test of the Fama–French SMB factor. The unit of analysis is the individual stock, and size enters through market-capitalisation rankings rather than a small-minus-big factor return series.

Data and design

Twenty years of monthly stock returns

The sample contains 40 randomly selected NYSE-listed companies with uninterrupted CRSP data from December 2002 to February 2023. Prices are adjusted for corporate actions and combined with shares outstanding to calculate market capitalisation. Each month, the firms are ranked into four equally sized groups, from the smallest ten companies to the largest ten.

The tier assignments are relatively stable: they change in 112 of 9,680 firm-month observations, compared with much more frequent movement in exact rankings. The dissertation therefore uses the four size tiers for its main comparisons and exact end-of-sample ranks as a sensitivity check.

Empirical model

Estimating return and conditional-variance dynamics

A stock-specific subset AR(2)-GARCH-in-Mean model is estimated for each company. The mean equation uses a sparse lag structure that retains the second monthly return lag alongside conditional variance. The variance equation separates the immediate response to a shock from the influence of past variance.

rt = γ0 + δrt−2 + cσt2 + at

at = σtεt

σt2 = α0 + α1at−12 + β1σt−12

Here, α1 captures sensitivity to recent squared innovations, while the combined α1 + β1 term is the more informative measure of volatility persistence. The coefficient c measures the loading of expected return on conditional variance; it should not be read as the total risk premium without also considering the level of variance.

Results

Strong volatility dynamics, weak evidence of a size gradient

Descriptive pattern

Smaller firms have higher average returns and return standard deviations in the sample.

Volatility clustering

Most stock-level shock-response and persistence estimates are statistically significant.

Ordered trend tests

The p-values are 0.3041 for α1 and 0.2451 for β1, so neither size trend is significant.

Rank sensitivity check

Using exact end-of-sample ranks, the lowest reported p-value is 0.1738 for β1.

Interpretation

The analysis finds evidence that volatility is persistent, but not that persistence changes monotonically with firm size. This is a failure to reject the no-trend hypothesis, not proof that size and volatility dynamics are unrelated in the wider market.

Extensions & robustness

How I would extend the study

The dissertation provides a baseline test of the relationship between firm size and volatility dynamics. A second version would examine whether its central finding remains stable under four extensions to the data, model and inference.

01 · Dynamic size measurement

Align size and volatility through time

Original choice. Each stock receives one set of GARCH parameters estimated from its full 2002–2023 return history, while the main comparisons use its February 2023 size tier. Broad tiers changed in only 112 of 9,680 firm-month intervals, making a stable classification appear reasonable.

Extension. A company may move between size categories during the estimation period. Matching rolling parameter estimates to contemporaneous or lagged size would capture that evolution more precisely than a single end-of-sample classification.

Approach. Match rolling parameter estimates to lagged market capitalisation, rebalance size groups annually, or use a panel specification in which size changes through time.

02 · Sample breadth

Broaden the cross-section

Original choice. Forty companies with uninterrupted monthly data produce a balanced and directly comparable panel across the full sample period.

Extension. This design focuses the result on established firms with continuous histories. A larger universe that also accommodates market entry and exit would broaden the result's relevance and increase the power of comparisons across size groups.

Approach. Draw from a broader CRSP universe, document the sampling and inclusion rules, and expand the cross-section before forming size groups.

03 · Model robustness

Test asymmetry and heavy tails

Original choice. A Gaussian GARCH(1,1) keeps estimation and cross-company comparison tractable. It is also a widely used benchmark for volatility persistence.

Extension. Equity returns can have heavy tails, and negative shocks may affect volatility more strongly than positive shocks. Alternative specifications provide a useful test of whether the estimated size pattern depends on the benchmark model.

Approach. Re-estimate with Student-t innovations and asymmetric GJR-GARCH or EGARCH specifications, then test whether any size conclusion survives across models.

04 · Inference

Carry parameter uncertainty into the second stage

Original choice. The Jonckheere–Terpstra test accommodates ordered size tiers, tied ranks and unknown parameter distributions without imposing a linear relationship.

Extension. The stock-level inputs are estimated with different levels of precision, while common market and sector shocks can affect several companies at once. Incorporating those features would make the cross-sectional evidence more informative.

Approach. Propagate first-stage uncertainty with a bootstrap or joint hierarchical model, add market and industry controls, and pre-specify a smaller set of primary parameters with an appropriate multiple-testing adjustment.
