Tax Design and the UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy

A theory-led analysis of tiered excise design through optimal commodity taxation, Pigouvian correction, producer incentives and distributional incidence.

UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy By Jiefu Zhang

The result in one view

The economic case for the SDIL is fundamentally incentive-based: tiered rates can internalise obesity-related costs and shift products towards lower sugar content, while the calibrated direct burden remains small relative to household expenditure. Revenue efficiency provides a benchmark for the analysis rather than defining the policy's primary objective.

Tax architecture

Which objective should the levy optimise?

The UK introduced the Soft Drinks Industry Levy in April 2018 as part of its response to childhood obesity. At the time examined in this project, drinks containing more than 5 but less than 8 grams of sugar per 100 millilitres faced an 18 pence-per-litre rate, while drinks containing at least 8 grams faced a 24 pence rate. Drinks at or below the lower threshold were exempt.

Rather than treating the levy as a single-purpose tax, the project separates four dimensions: the excess burden of raising revenue, the calibration of external costs, the distribution of payments across households and the behavioural incentives created by sugar-content thresholds.

Analytical framework

Four dimensions of tax design

Optimal-tax benchmark

How much excess burden accompanies each unit of revenue collected?

Pigouvian calibration

Distributional incidence

Behavioural architecture

How do discrete thresholds reshape pricing, consumption and product formulation?

Optimal-tax benchmark

Revenue efficiency is a benchmark, not the binding objective

The paper begins with a stylised market in which supply is perfectly elastic and the levy is passed through to consumers. With relatively elastic demand for sugar-sweetened beverages, a price increase produces a comparatively large quantity response and therefore a larger deadweight loss for a given amount raised.

The inverse-elasticity rule therefore exposes a trade-off between revenue and behavioural distortion. Its value here is diagnostic: it shows why the SDIL's economic rationale cannot be evaluated by receipts alone and must instead incorporate corrective and incentive effects.

Externality correction

Pigouvian logic anchors the policy rationale

The project calibrates an average obesity-related external cost of approximately £0.103 per litre of sugar-sweetened beverages. Combining aggregate obesity costs, an estimated attributable share and national drink volumes produces a first-pass benchmark against which the statutory rates can be interpreted.

Even with those limitations, the exercise clarifies why a tiered levy may be preferable to a flat charge. If expected harm rises with sugar concentration, higher rates for higher-sugar products can approximate that gradient while giving producers an incentive to reformulate.

Economic interpretation

Falling levy receipts need not indicate policy failure. If producers reduce sugar content to enter a lower tier or avoid the charge, the behavioural response is part of the intended policy mechanism rather than an incidental loss of revenue.

Distributional incidence

A visible incidence gradient, but a limited expenditure burden

The paper combines household soft-drink expenditure, an estimated weighted-average levy rate and total weekly spending across five groups. Within that calibration, the effective levy falls from approximately 0.227‰ of total spending in the lowest group to 0.088‰ in the highest.

The direction is regressive because similar drink volumes represent a larger share of a smaller household budget. The estimated magnitude, however, is modest. A complete welfare assessment would also consider differences in product choice, pass-through and health benefits rather than comparing tax payments alone.

Research agenda

From analytical calibration to causal evidence

The analytical framework identifies the mechanisms that matter. A natural empirical extension would estimate their relative contribution using behaviour observed around the policy's announcement, implementation and tier thresholds.

01 · Producer response

Model reformulation as a central policy channel

The tier thresholds give manufacturers a discrete incentive to reduce sugar without necessarily raising consumer prices or reducing drink volumes. Product-level data around the thresholds would separate reformulation from price and quantity responses.

02 · Demand and pass-through

Use UK-specific purchase and price data

Scanner or household-panel data could replace the benchmark foreign demand elasticity, equal product weights and full pass-through assumption. This would allow substitution between brands, drink categories and levy tiers to be estimated directly.

03 · External cost

Separate marginal social costs from private health costs

A more precise Pigouvian benchmark would distinguish costs borne by consumers from fiscal and other costs borne by society, and estimate how the external component changes with sugar content and consumption intensity.

04 · Household incidence

Measure distribution using contemporary microdata

Current UK household data with product-level sugar content would support tier-specific effective rates and heterogeneous pass-through. Combining payments with estimated health gains would also produce a fuller distributional welfare comparison.
