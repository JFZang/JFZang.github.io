# The AI Trade Has Moved from Equities to Credit

The decline in AI-related equities on 5 November looked sudden. It was not. The immediate trigger was the combination of weak Qualcomm earnings and the Democratic sweep of the US midterm elections, which raised the probability of tighter restrictions on data-centre development. The semiconductor index briefly fell by double digits and the Nasdaq lost approximately 5.5%, before recovering part of the decline on the following day. Yet the market had already been weakening since its early-October peak. The election converted a long-standing valuation and financing concern into a single crowded exit.

## The concern preceded the trigger

The third-quarter earnings season did not establish that demand for AI had disappeared. In many cases, revenue and earnings remained strong. The problem was the relationship between that demand and the capital required to satisfy it. Higher spending guidance ceased to be interpreted automatically as evidence of future growth and began to be treated as a claim on future free cash flow.

This distinction became particularly visible when strong operating results failed to support share prices. Meta raised the lower end of its 2026 capital-expenditure range and recorded its first negative quarterly free cash flow in more than a decade. Across Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle, estimated 2026 AI expenditure approached the cumulative amount invested since the release of ChatGPT. Investors were no longer asking whether AI revenue was growing. They were asking whether it was growing quickly enough to make the build-out self-financing.

The same reassessment had already appeared in credit. AI-related corporate issuance reached extraordinary volumes, with approximately $67 billion raised since late October alone. Investors demanded increasing concessions for long-dated hyperscaler debt, while some AI-related bonds traded at wider spreads than similarly rated investment-grade securities. The equity correction therefore followed a signal already present in the financing market: capital remained available, but no longer appeared unlimited or indifferent to price.

## Why government bonds rallied

The reversal in government bonds completed the picture. Before the sell-off, sovereign bonds had themselves been the source of concern as inflation, fiscal issuance and term premia pushed yields towards multi-decade highs. When AI equities fell, the same bonds recovered their traditional safe-haven role.

This does not mean that fiscal or inflation risk disappeared. It means that the dominant shock changed. A bond is unattractive when the market fears inflation and excessive duration supply, but valuable when the immediate concern becomes a decline in expected growth and risky-asset liquidity. The rally therefore represented a change in the relative hierarchy of risks rather than a resolution of the bond-market problem.

## What the midterms change

The election adds a constraint that earnings models had treated only lightly: the political cost of the AI infrastructure build-out. Data-centre opponents performed strongly in congressional, gubernatorial and local races. The principal objections concern electricity prices, water use, tax subsidies and the effect of large campuses on local infrastructure. A Democratic Congress is likely to conduct more hearings on AI governance, consumer protection and the allocation of power costs. At state level, permitting restrictions and conditions on utility investment could delay individual projects more directly.

The effect is not entirely negative. Greater regulatory clarity, grid investment and a more explicit allocation of infrastructure costs could reduce project uncertainty over time. Both parties also retain an interest in American leadership in AI. Divided government and the presidential veto limit the probability of sweeping federal legislation. The immediate risk is consequently not a national prohibition of data centres, but a slower, more expensive and geographically fragmented build-out.

## Temporary correction or structural repricing

Part of the decline was temporary. The speed of the move, forced reductions by leveraged investors and the partial rebound on 6 November all indicate a liquidation component. A sector can also recover sharply when positioning rather than earnings accounts for the marginal seller.

However, a return to the previous valuation regime requires more than another revenue beat. The market needs evidence that capital expenditure is approaching a plateau, that AI revenue is converting into free cash flow and that debt spreads can absorb further issuance without persistent widening. It also needs clarity on power availability and data-centre regulation following the elections.

The most likely outcome is therefore neither an immediate collapse of the AI investment cycle nor a simple restoration of the former trade. The physical demand for compute remains substantial, but the financing terms have changed. Companies with visible cash conversion, manageable external funding requirements and access to power should recover first. More leveraged data-centre developers, neoclouds and suppliers whose valuations require uninterrupted capacity expansion remain exposed.

The correction was triggered by an election and an earnings report. Its durability will be determined in the bond market.
