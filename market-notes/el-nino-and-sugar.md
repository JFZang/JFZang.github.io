# El Niño Is a Calendar, Not a Single Trade

The latest warning from the World Meteorological Organization is unusually categorical. El Niño is expected to strengthen into a very strong event towards the end of 2026, with a probability close to 100% that it will persist through February 2027. Its climatic effects may remain visible further into 2027. For commodity markets, however, persistence does not imply one simultaneous price shock. Each crop has a different biological and harvesting calendar, creating a sequence of potential catalysts rather than a single El Niño trade.

## India has moved from exporter to importer

Sugar provides the most immediate example. On 20 August, India authorised the duty-free import of one million tonnes of raw sugar, its first opening of this kind in almost a decade. The decision followed lower-than-expected production, weather-related damage and a decline in closing inventories to an estimated 3.9 million tonnes—25% below the preceding season and 40% below the five-year average. India had exported approximately 0.8 million tonnes during 2025/26; it has therefore moved from contributing marginal supply to creating additional import demand.

The distinction matters because India has historically acted as a swing supplier. When domestic output is sufficient, exports help balance the world market. When stocks become uncomfortable, government restrictions remove those exports; the present import quota reverses the flow altogether. Brazil must now absorb not only weaker Asian export availability but also part of India's import requirement.

Thailand remains a large net exporter, although its experience illustrates the production risk. Its sugar output fell by approximately 20% during the 2023–24 El Niño. Australia remains a structural exporter but has also experienced harvest delays, while Brazil has become increasingly important as the residual source of supply. The bullish sugar case is therefore not simply that El Niño reduces yields. It is that reduced output in India or Thailand now meets a market in which India has already crossed from export restriction into import demand.

## The catalyst calendar

| Window | What becomes observable | Principal exposures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **September–October 2026** | Completion of India's monsoon, allocation and arrival of its raw-sugar imports, and early estimates of cane yields | Sugar, rice and cotton |
| **November 2026–January 2027** | Peak El Niño, start of Indian and Thai crushing, Vietnamese Robusta harvest and West African cocoa main-crop arrivals | Sugar, Robusta coffee and cocoa |
| **February–April 2027** | Confirmation of Asian sugar output and Indian trade policy; Southeast Asian dry-season stress; Brazilian cane-development conditions | Sugar, natural rubber and coffee |
| **May–September 2027** | Lagged effect of 2026 dryness on oil-palm fruit yields and perennial crops; clearer evidence on the 2027 coffee and rubber supply response | Palm oil, Robusta coffee and natural rubber |

The lag is particularly important for palm oil. Dry weather first affects flowering and fruit formation, while the reduction in fresh-fruit bunches may not appear for six to twelve months. Rubber and coffee are also perennial crops, so damage to trees or flowering can affect output after the atmospheric event itself has weakened. Sugar reacts sooner because crushing data provide relatively rapid confirmation of cane availability and sugar recovery rates.

## What has already been priced

The curves show recognition of the risk, but not a uniform judgement that a lasting shortage has arrived. As of early September:

- **ICE No. 11 sugar** rose from approximately 14 cents per pound in early 2026 to 18.07 cents for October 2026. March 2027 traded at 19.05 cents, before May and July declined to 18.44 and 18.06 cents. The curve is therefore in contango through March, followed by backwardation. It prices the greatest tightness around the Asian crop-confirmation window rather than a permanent shortage.
- **Malaysian crude palm oil** displayed a clearer contango, rising from approximately MYR4,608 per tonne for September 2026 to MYR5,155 for January 2027, with deferred 2027 prices remaining elevated. This is consistent with a market assigning value to the delayed biological effect of dry weather.
- **Robusta coffee** was only slightly in contango from September to November 2026—approximately $3,400 to $3,430 per tonne—before declining gradually across 2027. The curve recognises near-term uncertainty but still assumes that available Vietnamese and Brazilian supply will prevent a prolonged deficit.
- **London cocoa** rose from approximately £4,773 per tonne for September 2026 to a peak near £4,983 in March 2027, before moving into backwardation. Like sugar, it concentrates the premium around the period in which the 2026/27 crop becomes measurable.

None of this establishes that El Niño is fully priced. A futures curve contains storage, financing, seasonality and current physical tightness as well as weather expectations; contango cannot be read mechanically as a forecast of higher spot prices. Moreover, markets can price the probability of adverse weather before they know its location, timing or effect on yields. The known existence of El Niño is substantially priced. The eventual crop loss is not.

## Investment implications and cautions

Sugar appears to have the clearest near-term catalyst because India's trade reversal is already observable and the next Indian and Thai crops will be tested within months. Palm oil provides a later and potentially more persistent exposure, but its pronounced contango creates a negative roll yield for a passive long position. Robusta and rubber may offer later optionality if Southeast Asian dryness persists into early 2027. Cocoa has considerable weather sensitivity, but its high volatility and existing risk premium make it less clean as a new El Niño position.

The principal caution is Brazil. Strong Brazilian sugar production offset much of the Asian shortfall during the previous El Niño. The sugar–ethanol allocation, Brazilian real, port capacity and Center-South rainfall may consequently matter as much as conditions in India. Substitution also matters: users can switch between palm, soybean and other vegetable oils, while manufacturers can reformulate or hedge their sugar requirements.

The appropriate question is therefore not whether El Niño has been priced. It is which part of the loss distribution has been priced at each maturity. The first test is India's import execution and the Asian cane crush. The second is whether Southeast Asian dryness persists long enough to reach palm oil, rubber and coffee production in 2027. A broad long-commodity position obscures this sequence; exposure should instead be matched to the crop and contract month in which the physical evidence will arrive.
