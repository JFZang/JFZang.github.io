# El Niño Is Certain; the Commodity Losses Are Not

Meteorological uncertainty has narrowed considerably. The World Meteorological Organization expects El Niño to intensify towards a peak around December 2026, while NOAA assigns an 83% probability that the event will reach a historically exceptional threshold during October–December. The commodity question is no longer whether El Niño exists. It is whether the resulting crop losses exceed the weather premium already embedded in prices.

## What the academic evidence actually says

The empirical literature supports a relationship between ENSO and some tropical commodity prices, but not a uniform El Niño effect. Research published in the *American Journal of Agricultural Economics* finds that sea-surface-temperature anomalies improve the prediction of prices for a subset of agriculturally traded commodities. The result is selective: geography, inventories, substitution and the timing of crop development determine whether a climate signal becomes a price signal.

Oil palm provides the clearest example of a delayed effect. A recent study using monthly records submitted by Malaysian estates finds statistically significant yield reductions associated with extreme ENSO exposure across lags extending from zero to 23 months. Under one high-exposure calibration, cumulative fresh-fruit-bunch and oil yields were approximately 9% and 8% below their respective means. This biological lag explains why El Niño may affect 2027 production even if ocean temperatures peak in late 2026.

The evidence for sugar is less mechanical. A systematic review of Indo-Pacific studies reports negative effects on some crops, but no statistically clear effect on Thai sugarcane yields in the studies it examined. Local temperature, rainfall during cane elongation, milling recovery rates and policy responses may matter more than the ENSO label itself. This is an important caution against interpreting every rise in sugar prices as confirmation of El Niño damage.

## What the market has priced

**Sugar has priced the immediate risk most aggressively.** The World Bank's October benchmark was approximately 26.5% higher than a year earlier, while heavy rainfall disrupted harvesting and processing in Brazil's Centre-South. ICE No. 11 traded near 18 cents per pound in early November, well above the 13–14 cent lows reached earlier in 2026. The March 2027 contract stood above May, and the curve moved into backwardation further out. The market has therefore priced near-term tightness, Indian trade restrictions and some Brazilian weather risk. It has not priced a confirmed multi-year shortage.

**Palm oil has priced the lag rather than a current deficit.** Malaysian inventories remain high and November physical prices weakened towards MYR4,100 per tonne. Yet the futures curve rose from approximately MYR4,126 for November 2026 to MYR4,587 for April 2027 on 5 November. This contango is unusually informative: prompt supply is comfortable, while deferred contracts carry a premium for seasonal tightening and possible El Niño damage. The direction of the risk is priced; the eventual size of the yield loss is not.

**Robusta coffee has priced relatively little persistent damage.** November 2026 traded above January 2027 in early November, leaving the front of the curve in backwardation as Vietnam entered its harvest. Current export availability and the prospect of Brazilian supply still dominate deferred weather concerns. A persistent rainfall deficit during flowering and bean development would therefore require a more material repricing of 2027 contracts than is presently visible.

**Natural rubber remains the least clean expression.** Academic work identifies a channel from ENSO rainfall anomalies to Indonesian rubber productivity and exports, but the traded market is less liquid and more fragmented than sugar, coffee or palm oil. Chinese industrial demand, synthetic-rubber prices and crude oil can overwhelm the weather signal. It is better treated as a confirmation indicator than as the primary El Niño trade.

## What remains unpriced

Three uncertainties cannot yet be converted into a settled price. First, the spatial distribution of rainfall matters more than the headline Niño index: a record Pacific anomaly does not guarantee that the most important producing districts receive damaging weather. Second, perennial crops respond with lags, so current inventories can conceal a production loss that becomes visible only during 2027. Third, producers and governments respond. Brazil can adjust the sugar–ethanol mix, India can alter trade policy and food manufacturers can substitute between vegetable oils or coffee grades.

The next confirmation windows are consequently crop-specific. Indian and Thai cane-crushing data will test the sugar thesis during the northern winter. Malaysian and Indonesian rainfall, followed by fresh-fruit-bunch yields, will test palm oil during the first half of 2027. Vietnamese flowering and export data will determine whether Robusta moves from a current-harvest market to a weather-shortage market.

El Niño itself is fully recognised. Sugar has priced much of the near-term risk, palm oil has priced a deferred risk and coffee has priced comparatively little lasting damage. What remains open is not the climate forecast, but the conversion of that forecast into physical tonnes.
