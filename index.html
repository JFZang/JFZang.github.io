Working vocabulary

Glossary

A growing collection of useful terms I come across in research and reading—kept in plain English, with a short note on how I interpret each one.

Earn-out Contingent acquisition payment
Simple explanation

A portion of an acquisition price that is paid only if the acquired business meets agreed post-deal targets, such as revenue, EBITDA, customer retention or product milestones.

Earn-out vs contingent consideration

An earn-out is generally a type of contingent consideration tied to the acquired business's future performance. Contingent consideration is the broader accounting term and can include acquisition payments triggered by other future events or conditions.

Why it matters

Earn-outs can bridge a valuation gap between buyer and seller and reduce the buyer's upfront risk, but they may also encourage short-term target optimisation or create post-deal disputes. For accounting analysis, liability-classified contingent consideration is generally remeasured after acquisition, with fair-value changes affecting earnings; equity-classified amounts are generally not remeasured.

Interpretation

Check the performance metric, threshold, measurement period, maximum payout and accounting classification. Payments linked to a seller's continued employment may be treated as compensation expense rather than purchase consideration, which changes both the acquisition accounting and post-deal earnings profile.

Analytical treatment

Exclude fair-value remeasurement gains or losses on contingent consideration from normalised operating earnings because they do not represent the underlying operating performance of the current period.

  • Do not ignore the contingent consideration itself.
  • Retain expected cash payments as acquisition cost, a liability or a future cash outflow, as appropriate.
  • Reflect expected share issuance in potential dilution.
Exclude accounting remeasurement volatility from operating profit, but retain the real economic cost in enterprise value, cash-flow and dilution analysis.
Incremental economics Economics of additional growth
Simple explanation

The revenue, profit and cash return generated by the next unit of growth relative to the additional cost and capital required to produce it. Unlike average economics, it focuses on what changes at the margin.

Simplified formulas

Incremental margin is not self-defining: the profit measure used in the numerator must be stated.

Incremental gross margin = Δ Gross profit Δ Revenue
Incremental operating margin = Δ Operating profit Δ Revenue
Incremental ROIC = Δ NOPAT Δ Invested capital
Why it matters

Company-wide averages describe the economics of the existing business, but incremental economics reveal whether new growth is becoming more or less valuable. Improving incremental margins can indicate operating leverage and scalability; weak incremental returns may show that growth requires disproportionate spending or capital.

Interpretation

Use comparable periods and separate organic changes from acquisitions, disposals, currency, cyclicality and unusual items. A strong incremental margin does not by itself prove value creation: the additional capital required, the durability of the growth and the resulting incremental cash return also matter.

NOL Net Operating Loss
Simple explanation

A tax loss arising when a company's allowable tax deductions exceed its taxable income for a period. Subject to the relevant tax rules, an NOL may be carried forward or back to offset taxable income in other periods and reduce cash taxes.

Simplified formula
Potential tax benefit ≈ Usable NOL × Applicable tax rate
Why it matters

NOLs can shelter future taxable profits and therefore have economic value, but their headline balance is not the same as a readily realisable asset. Expiry dates, annual utilisation limits, ownership changes, jurisdictional restrictions and the company's ability to generate suitable taxable income can reduce the amount ultimately used.

Interpretation

Separate the gross NOL balance from the recognised deferred tax asset and from the expected cash-tax benefit. Value only the portion likely to be used, in the correct jurisdictions and within the permitted period, discounted for the timing of the resulting tax savings.

NRR Net Revenue Retention
Simple explanation

The percentage of recurring revenue retained from an existing customer cohort after expansion, contraction and churn, excluding revenue from new customers.

Formula
NRR = Starting recurring revenue + Expansion − Contraction − Churn Starting recurring revenue × 100%
Why SaaS uses it

NRR is most commonly used for SaaS and other subscription businesses because their growth depends not only on acquiring new customers, but also on retaining and expanding recurring revenue from existing customers. By excluding new customers, NRR isolates whether the existing customer base is growing on its own through renewals, price increases and cross-selling. It is less meaningful for transactional or project-based businesses where customer spending is irregular and non-recurring.

Interpretation

NRR above 100% means expansion from retained customers more than offsets lost or reduced business. It is a useful indicator of product value, customer stickiness and the efficiency of growth, but the cohort definition and calculation period should be checked before comparing companies.

PPA Purchase Price Allocation
Simple explanation

The accounting process used after an acquisition to allocate the purchase price across the identifiable assets acquired and liabilities assumed at fair value. Any residual amount is generally recorded as goodwill.

Simplified formula
Purchase price ≈ Fair value of identifiable net assets + Goodwill
Why it matters

PPA determines how an acquisition affects reported earnings after the deal closes. Allocating more value to finite-lived intangible assets—such as customer relationships, technology or brands—creates future amortisation expense, while allocating more to goodwill avoids scheduled amortisation but may increase later impairment risk.

Interpretation

Investors should examine PPA when comparing reported and adjusted earnings, assessing whether an acquisition is genuinely accretive, and judging the quality of management's deal accounting. Large goodwill balances, short assumed asset lives or repeated exclusions of acquisition-related amortisation can materially change the apparent profitability and return on invested capital of an acquisitive company.

RPO Remaining Performance Obligations
Simple explanation

The contracted revenue a company expects to recognise in the future for goods or services it has not yet delivered. It generally combines deferred revenue with contracted amounts not yet invoiced.

Simplified formula
RPO ≈ Deferred revenue + Contracted amounts not yet billed
RPO vs AR

Accounts receivable (AR) represents revenue already recognised and invoiced, but not yet collected in cash. RPO represents contracted revenue that has not yet been recognised because the remaining performance obligations have not been fulfilled.

Interpretation

RPO is most useful for assessing forward revenue visibility, backlog quality and changes in customer commitment—especially in subscription and long-term contract businesses. Current RPO, expected to be recognised within 12 months, is usually more relevant to near-term growth. RPO is not automatically a revenue forecast: timing, cancellations, contract duration, acquisitions and billing structure can all affect it.

Valuation allowance Deferred tax asset valuation allowance
Simple explanation

A reserve that reduces a deferred tax asset to the amount expected to be realised. Under US GAAP, it is recorded when the weight of available evidence indicates that some or all of the deferred tax asset is more likely than not to remain unused.

US GAAP presentation
Net deferred tax asset = Gross deferred tax asset − Valuation allowance
Connection to NOLs

NOL carryforwards often create deferred tax assets. If the company lacks sufficient evidence that it will generate taxable income in time to use them, a valuation allowance reduces the recognised net asset. The NOL still legally exists; the allowance reflects uncertainty over its accounting realisation.

Framework difference

IFRS has no directly equivalent account or formal term corresponding to a valuation allowance. Under IAS 12, the same uncertainty is addressed at the recognition stage: a deferred tax asset arising from unused tax losses or credits is recognised only to the extent that sufficient future taxable profit or suitable reversing taxable temporary differences are considered probable. Any remaining potential tax benefit stays unrecognised rather than being recorded gross and offset by a separate allowance.

IFRS recognised DTA = Portion whose utilisation is probable

The unrecognised portion is reassessed at each reporting date. It may be recognised later if recovery becomes probable; conversely, an already recognised deferred tax asset is reduced if sufficient future taxable profit is no longer expected. The economic outcome can therefore resemble a valuation allowance, but the terminology, recognition sequence and balance-sheet presentation differ.

Interpretation

Creating an allowance usually increases tax expense, while releasing one usually creates a tax benefit; both are generally non-cash in the period recorded. A release can signal improved expectations for future taxable income, but it should not automatically be treated as recurring operating profit or fresh economic value—the underlying tax attribute existed before the accounting reassessment.
