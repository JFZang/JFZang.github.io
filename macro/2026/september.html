Monthly Macro Highlights

September compares the Federal Reserve's rate increase with the Bank of England's hold, then examines US spending, UK service-sector composition, Treasury and sterling moves, and China's shift from land finance towards central funding.

September 2026Reporting window: 22 August–25 September 2026By Jiefu Zhang

Central question

Why did the Federal Reserve raise rates while the Bank of England held, when both were facing another energy-driven rise in inflation?

Headline inflation alone did not decide either meeting. US activity strengthened, while UK services inflation remained stable and higher market rates had already passed into household and business lending. The Fed therefore saw more domestic demand alongside inflation; the Bank saw an imported shock entering an economy where borrowing conditions were already tightening.

Evidence and mechanism

The same energy shock met different domestic conditions

Energy was the common starting point. Brent crude and UK wholesale gas had risen 36% and 78%, respectively, since the period before the Bank's July report. In the US, petrol rose 3.9% in August and accounted for more than one-third of the monthly CPI increase; UK motor fuels made the largest contribution to its rise in annual inflation. [Bank of England, 17 September] [BLS CPI, 11 September]

The shock worked in two directions. It raised prices but reduced the income available for other purchases. If households cut spending and firms absorb costs, the initial inflation can fade; if demand remains firm, firms can pass on costs and workers can seek higher wages. A rate rise cannot produce more oil. It can only lean against this second-round process while adding to the income squeeze.

The income effect also differed. The UK imported a net 43.5% of its energy in 2025, while the US was a net exporter. US consumers still paid more, but part of the payment became domestic producer income; more of the UK increase left the economy. This did not decide policy, but it made the oil move a clearer national-income squeeze in the UK. [UK Energy Trends] [US EIA, 27 May]

Existing rates reached households at different speeds. UK mortgage fixes commonly expire after two or five years, while more than 95% of US mortgages have fixed rates and usually longer terms. Each UK refinancing cohort adds to the squeeze; more existing US borrowers remain insulated. [IMF, mortgage structures]

The policy question was therefore which side of the shock was becoming more important. The Fed's revised outlook and spending data suggested that demand had not yet weakened enough to contain wider pass-through. UK inflation data showed less evidence of that pass-through while existing borrowing costs continued to tighten demand. [Federal Reserve projections, 16 September] [ONS CPI, 16 September]

Market and financial conditions

US policy expectations rose while UK conditions tightened without a rate increase

US Treasury curve

The two-year yield rose 63 basis points and the ten-year yield rose 48.

10-year TIPS yield: +47bp

The move nearly matched the increase in the nominal ten-year yield.

Sterling

It fell 2.6% against the dollar but only 0.5% against the euro.

UK two-year mortgage quote: +95bp

The increase occurred without another rise in Bank Rate.

The nominal ten-year yield rose 48 basis points and the ten-year TIPS yield rose 47 basis points, leaving ten-year inflation compensation almost unchanged. The two-year yield rose 15 basis points more than the ten-year yield, suggesting that the repricing was concentrated towards the policy-sensitive part of the curve. TIPS yields also contain a real term premium, so they are not a pure forecast of future policy rates. [FRED, US 2-year yield] [FRED, US 10-year yield] [FRED, 10-year TIPS yield]

Sterling's much smaller fall against the euro suggests a dollar move rather than a broad sterling sell-off, although it still raised the pound cost of oil. The quoted two-year mortgage rate was about 95 basis points above its pre-conflict level. The Bank's hold therefore did not stop tightening through the currency and refinancing channels. [Bank of England exchange rates, 24 September] [Bank of England, 17 September]

The S&P 500 rose 0.7% and the high-yield spread widened by 11 basis points, so the higher Fed path was not accompanied by a sharp repricing of near-term equity or credit risk. [FRED, S&P 500] [FRED, high-yield spread]

United States

Demand remained broad

Broad spending made the energy shock harder for the Fed to ignore

August retail and food-services sales rose 1.2%. Petrol-station sales increased 3.1%, but sales excluding petrol still rose 1.1% and sales excluding both petrol and vehicles rose 1.2%. Online retailers gained 2.6% and food services 1.2%, so the improvement extended beyond fuel. [US Census retail sales, 16 September]

These are current-dollar sales rather than quantities, but that distinction does not make them irrelevant to the Fed. An energy shock is more likely to spread when households continue spending elsewhere and firms do not have to choose between passing on costs and losing sales. August did not prove that wider pass-through had begun, but it removed one reason to wait: the higher petrol bill had not yet displaced spending across the rest of retail. [BLS CPI, 11 September]

The employment revision reinforced the same point. In August, the Fed had faced the possibility that weak hiring would reduce household income and contain the energy shock by itself. Once July's reported decline became a gain and August employment increased, that automatic slowdown looked less reliable. Combined with the breadth of retail spending, the revision gave the Fed a domestic reason to respond to an external shock.

My view

I would treat the September increase as insurance, not the start of an automatic hiking cycle. After acting on pass-through risk, the Fed should require clearer evidence in wages or non-energy prices before moving again. Repeatedly responding to headline energy inflation would deepen the income squeeze that may eventually cool demand without further action.

United Kingdom

Business services, weak household signal

Business services lifted measured output without strengthening the case for a rate rise

Services accounted for 81.2% of UK gross value added in 2024, but the label covers very different activities. The ONS estimated that output rose 0.4% in the three months to July compared with the previous three months, as services grew 0.6% while production and construction each fell 0.5%. This was a timely, output-based estimate subject to revision, not evidence that the UK had entered a broad or durable expansion. [ONS Blue Book, 20 August] [ONS GDP, 11 September]

The stronger areas were mainly business and digital services: professional, scientific and technical activities grew 2.1%, and information and communication grew 2.5%. Consumer-facing services rose only 0.5% over the three months and fell 0.4% in July. Business projects and contracts do not move with weekly retail purchases, so these sectors can support measured output without implying equally strong household demand.

This composition also changes the inflation signal. Labour is a large cost for services, so a lasting domestic inflation problem should appear in wages and service prices. For now, business-services output increased while consumer-facing activity weakened and aggregate services inflation remained stable. The data therefore showed resilience in part of the service economy, not the broad domestic demand that would have strengthened the case for another rate rise.

My view

I would not treat the 6–3 vote as evidence that the Bank is simply one meeting behind the Fed. Faster mortgage repricing means that the same external shock can justify a different Bank Rate path. A prolonged hold would therefore be consistent with the same inflation target, not evidence that the Bank was more tolerant of inflation.

China

Land finance still shrinking

The question after property is who finances demand

Property supported demand through several balance sheets: households borrowed to buy, developers used presales and credit to build, and local governments sold land to spend. The last channel kept weakening. In January–August, local government-fund revenue fell 22.9% and land-sale revenue 28.6%. Local public-budget spending rose only 0.3%, compared with 6.0% for central-government spending. [China Ministry of Finance, 18 September]

This is why technology is not a direct replacement. It can replace part of the lost capital spending and raise productive capacity, but it does not reproduce the links between household credit, developer funding and local fiscal revenue. Technology may improve productivity and exports while the domestic financing gap left by property remains.

The replacement mechanism is therefore more likely to involve a change in who borrows. The 2026 budget includes RMB 1.3 trillion of ultra-long special government bonds, with RMB 1.06 trillion transferred to local governments. This moves financing towards the central balance sheet, but it does not determine the final demand created. A public project supports construction and suppliers; paying arrears repairs private-company cash flow; transfers and social spending reach households. The same funding can therefore produce different recoveries. [China Ministry of Finance, 2026 central budget]

My view

This would make demand more dependent on repeated budget decisions and less self-reinforcing. I would treat central financing as stabilisation rather than a new demand engine until private orders and household income respond.
