Monthly Macro Highlights

Energy has changed
the policy conversation

Growth is still holding together, but the renewed rise in energy costs has made the inflation outlook less benign and the path to lower rates less direct.

June 2026Reporting window: 23 May–25 June 2026By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

The key change in June is not a collapse in demand. It is the return of a supply constraint. Higher energy prices are lifting headline inflation, squeezing real incomes and forcing central banks to keep one eye on second-round effects even as parts of the economy slow.

Global

The shock is uneven, but few economies are untouched

The World Bank now expects global growth to slow from 2.9% in 2025 to 2.5% in 2026. It has downgraded forecasts for roughly two-thirds of economies, citing disruption to commodity markets from the conflict in the Middle East. AI-related investment offsets part of the drag, but does not remove it. [World Bank, 11 June]

Energy importers face the clearest pressure through household bills, transport costs and tighter financial conditions. Exporters receive a terms-of-trade benefit, although that advantage can be lost if global demand weakens.

My view

I do not see the energy shock as an automatic recession signal. Its duration matters more than the initial move. A short spike would mainly affect headline inflation; a persistent one would reach wages, margins and investment plans. I prefer companies that can absorb higher input costs without relying on aggressive price increases.

Policy and market implications

Lower oil is helpful; the policy scar remains

Energy prices have fallen from their initial shock but remain above pre-conflict levels and volatile. The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% in June, with two members voting to raise it to 4%; the Federal Reserve also left its target range unchanged. [Bank of England, 18 June] [Federal Reserve, 17 June]

The two UK votes for a rise are a reminder that market pricing depends on second-round effects, not simply today’s barrel price. Falling oil can improve risk appetite quickly, but central banks need evidence from wages and services before treating the shock as finished.

United States

Firm demand, higher hurdle

The labour market gives the Fed room to wait

Payrolls rose by 172,000 in May, unemployment held at 4.3%, and average hourly earnings were 3.4% higher than a year earlier. Hiring was led by leisure and hospitality, local government and health care. [BLS, 5 June]

May CPI rose 0.5% on the month and 4.2% over the year. Energy accounted for more than sixty percent of the monthly increase, while core CPI rose a more moderate 0.2%. [BLS, 10 June] The Fed kept its target range at 3.50–3.75% on 17 June and described activity as solid while noting elevated inflation. [Federal Reserve, 17 June]

My view

The Fed can afford patience because employment has not broken. The useful distinction is between energy-driven headline inflation and broader pressure in services and wages. If the latter remain contained, holding rates steady may be enough. If energy begins to alter wage demands, the bar for easing rises quickly.

United Kingdom

Income squeeze risk

Momentum has softened just as energy risk returns

UK GDP fell 0.1% in April after two stronger months, although output still grew 0.7% across the three months to April. The monthly fall came from services, while construction edged higher. [ONS, 12 June]

CPI inflation was 2.8% in May. Underlying pressure had eased, but the benefit from earlier wholesale energy declines sits uneasily beside the newer rise in global fuel costs. [ONS, 17 June]

My view

The UK has less room than the US to absorb another real-income shock. Growth is modest and service-led, while households remain sensitive to mortgage and utility costs. I would not extrapolate April’s contraction into a downturn, but I would be cautious about consumer businesses with thin margins and high fixed costs.

China

Industry resilient

Production recovers, while consumers step back

Industrial value added grew 4.5% year on year in May, up from 4.1% in April. Electronics and equipment manufacturing remained stronger than many traditional industries. [NBS industrial production]

Retail sales fell 0.6% from a year earlier, though sales excluding autos rose 1.1%. Online services grew faster than goods across the first five months. [NBS retail sales]

My view

May is weaker than April on the household side: total retail sales have moved from near-stagnation to contraction, even as output improves. Autos explain part of the fall, but the gap still suggests that factories are responding to external or policy-supported demand more than to a confident consumer. Imported energy adds pressure because firms may have little room to pass higher costs on at home. I would look for a recovery in retail sales excluding autos before reading stronger production as a healthier domestic cycle.

What to watch

Four tests for the next month

Oil persistence

Contracts, wage negotiations and budgets react with lags. The relevant question is whether lower spot prices arrive before firms lock in a higher cost base.

US core prices

Benign core readings would show that energy has remained separate from domestic inflation. Transport and service categories are the likely bridge.

UK households

Retail volumes, arrears and consumer credit will show whether higher bills are reducing demand or merely changing its composition.

China demand

Stronger consumer confidence and retail breadth would be more reassuring than another production gain, which could add to excess supply.
