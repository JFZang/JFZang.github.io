Monthly Macro Highlights

June 2026 Macro Highlights

Oil prices fell sharply in June, but the latest official data still reflected the earlier increase. Import, producer and consumer prices showed how much of that increase had reached households and how much firms were still absorbing.

June 2026Reporting window: 23 May–25 June 2026By Jiefu Zhang

Central question

Who absorbed the change in commodity prices—consumers, company profits or exporters?

US households paid the direct increase in energy prices quickly. Importers and manufacturers absorbed part of the wider rise because their costs increased faster than their selling prices in the UK and China. Exporters recovered some of the cost by charging more, but not enough to match the rise in US import prices. Oil fell in June, although that relief had not yet reached the official data.

What changed this month

Oil fell before lower costs appeared in company data

Brent: −28.2%

Spot Brent fell from USD 102.75 on 26 May to USD 73.74 on 25 June.

US import prices: +1.9% m/m

May import prices rose again, led by a 12.5% increase in fuel imports and a 0.8% rise excluding fuel.

UK input PPI: +8.7% y/y

Factory input prices rose more than twice as fast as output prices, which increased 4.0%.

China purchase prices: +5.8% y/y

Prices paid by producers rose faster than their 3.9% factory-gate price increase.

Evidence and mechanism

Households and firms paid different parts of the higher bill

US households saw the direct energy increase first. May CPI rose 0.5%, and energy increased 3.9%, accounting for more than 60% of the monthly rise. Costs rose even faster for some companies: final-demand producer prices increased 1.1%, final-demand goods 2.8% and imported fuel 12.5%. [BLS CPI, 10 June] [BLS PPI, 11 June] [BLS trade prices, 16 June]

Both the 1.1% PPI increase and the 1.9% import-price increase were seasonally adjusted monthly changes. They capture the sharp May step-up, not a twelve-month inflation rate. A commodity shock can produce a large monthly rate without establishing the same pace for the year.

US exporters recovered some of the higher cost by charging more. Export prices rose 1.3% in May and prices for non-agricultural industrial supplies rose 2.4%. Total import prices still increased 1.9%, including 0.8% outside fuel. An importer selling into a competitive domestic market then had three main choices: raise customer prices, accept lower profits or change its suppliers and sales volumes.

The UK and China showed this difference more directly. UK input producer prices were 8.7% above a year earlier in May, compared with a 4.0% rise in selling prices; import prices rose 10.1%. In China, purchasing prices rose 5.8% while factory-gate prices rose 3.9%, with much larger increases in fuel, chemicals and non-ferrous metals. The baskets are different, so these figures cannot calculate profit margins exactly. They still suggest that manufacturers did not pass the full increase in costs to their customers. [ONS PPI, 17 June] [NBS PPI, 11 June]

Answer

Consumers paid the direct energy increase quickly. Importers and producers absorbed more of the indirect cost because their input prices rose faster than their selling prices, while exporters recovered part of it through higher export prices. June's fall in oil should reduce the pressure, but older inventories, existing contracts and regulated prices meant that the benefit would arrive at different times.

United States

Energy reached households first

US households saw fuel costs before other goods prices changed

Energy accounted for more than 60% of May's CPI increase, with gasoline up 7.0% in the month. Core CPI rose only 0.2%. At the same time, fuel import prices rose 12.5%, nonfuel import prices 0.8% and final-demand goods producer prices 2.8%. Households paid the direct fuel increase, while firms were still dealing with much of the remaining rise in costs.

May real disposable income and real consumer spending each rose 0.3%, while the personal saving rate was 3.0%. Households had not stopped spending, but the low saving rate left limited room to absorb repeated energy increases without changing other purchases. [BEA, 25 June]

My view

US households paid the visible energy increase quickly, while firms outside energy did not pass on all of their higher costs. Lower oil should help the next inflation reading. What I wanted to know was whether households would spend the saving or use it to rebuild the cash they had lost during the price spike.

United Kingdom

Costs rose faster than prices

UK firms' costs rose faster than their selling prices

May input producer prices were 8.7% above a year earlier, compared with 4.0% for output prices. Import prices were up 10.1%, and crude-oil inputs were still 71.8% higher than a year earlier despite falling 5.9% in May. [ONS PPI, 17 June]

This did not mean that every manufacturer earned less. Different products, hedges, inventories and productivity could change the result. But firms clearly had not passed every cost increase to consumers. Some would accept lower profits, sell fewer goods, change suppliers or raise prices later.

My view

The UK figures suggested that firms were absorbing part of the higher commodity bill in their profits. Lower spot prices would help only after older inventory and hedging contracts expired. Before assuming that households had benefited, I wanted to see whether companies stopped warning about profits and whether selling prices began to slow across more products.

China

Processors faced higher costs

Raw materials became more expensive faster than finished goods

In May, purchasing prices paid by industrial producers rose 5.8% from a year earlier, while producer selling prices rose 3.9%. Mining prices increased 15.8% and raw-material prices 9.2%, compared with 2.3% for processing. Fuel and power purchases rose 10.0%, chemical raw materials 11.8%, and non-ferrous metals and cables 22.0%. [NBS PPI, 11 June]

My view

Commodity suppliers were able to charge more, while manufacturers further down the chain could not raise their own prices by as much. That difference was likely to reduce profits for some processors. The next useful data would be profits at different stages of production rather than another comparison between industrial output and retail sales.

Underfollowed indicator

The gap between input and selling prices gives a clue about profits

Headline CPI says what consumers eventually paid, while a commodity quote says what traded today. The difference between input and output producer prices sits between them. A widening gap suggests that producers are absorbing more of the shock or delaying pass-through; a narrowing gap can come from lower inputs, higher selling prices or both. The cause matters for profits and for the next inflation reading.

Since last month

May's borrowing pressure was joined by higher company costs

What continued

Credit markets remained calm even as producer costs rose sharply.

What changed

Costs rose faster than selling prices, so some firms were absorbing the difference.

Still unclear

The June oil fall was too recent to know whether it would improve profits, lower prices or accompany weaker demand.

What would change my view

Selling prices rising as fast as costs without lower sales would show that firms could pass on more of the increase.
