Monthly Macro Highlights

The World Bank cut its global forecast as energy disruption persisted; US housing starts fell sharply, UK promotions lifted retail volumes, and Chinese private investment contracted.

June 2026Reporting window: 23 May–25 June 2026By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

June moves beyond inflation and payrolls. The World Bank marked down global growth, US housing starts dropped while permits barely changed, UK promotions lifted retail volumes, and Chinese equipment purchases rose as private investment fell.

Global

The World Bank marks down growth as energy costs rise

The World Bank expected global growth to slow from 2.9% in 2025 to 2.5% in 2026 and downgraded roughly two-thirds of economies, citing commodity-market disruption from the Middle East conflict. The forecast turned March’s price shock into a lower expectation for sales and production volumes. [World Bank, 11 June]

Energy importers face weaker household purchasing power, higher transport costs and tighter financial conditions. Exporters receive better terms of trade, but that benefit can fade if their customers cut demand. The same commodity move therefore redistributes income before it changes aggregate global growth.

My view

The downgrade matters because importers now face higher input bills and weaker expected final demand at the same time. Exporters gain revenue only while their customers keep buying. I expect the pressure to appear first in transport-heavy and price-sensitive company margins, before it is clear in aggregate employment data.

Policy and market implications

Lower spot energy does not end the inflation test

The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% in June, with two members voting to raise it to 4%. The Federal Reserve also left its target range unchanged. [Bank of England, 18 June] [Federal Reserve, 17 June]

The S&P 500 fell about 2.1% during the reporting window, from 7,519.12 on 26 May to 7,357.49 on 25 June. [FRED, S&P 500] That is a modest repricing after the April–May rally, not the kind of fall that by itself signals a funding shock.

My view

I would not use lower spot energy or a modest equity decline to declare the episode over. The policy question is whether transport costs spread into wages and services; the market question is whether weaker growth reaches earnings and credit. Neither was fully confirmed at the cut-off.

United States

Housing construction drops

Housing starts fall while permits hold steadier

Privately owned housing starts fell 15.4% in May to an annualised rate of 1.177 million and were 8.7% below a year earlier. Single-family starts fell only 1.9%, so much of the monthly decline came from the more volatile multi-family segment. [US Census and HUD, 16 June]

Building permits were much steadier at 1.413 million, down 0.7% from April and 0.2% from a year earlier. Units authorised but not yet started increased 4.7% in the month, leaving a larger gap between approvals and work under way. [US Census and HUD, 16 June]

My view

The data do not show a uniform housing collapse: permits barely changed and single-family starts were broadly flat, while multi-family construction drove the headline fall. They do show a conversion risk. If authorised projects continue to wait rather than break ground, construction spending and demand for building materials will weaken later in the year.

United Kingdom

Promotion-led spending

Promotions lift retail volumes after weak GDP

GDP fell 0.1% in April, although output grew 0.7% over the latest three months. CPI inflation was 2.8% in May. [ONS GDP, 12 June] [ONS CPI, 17 June]

Retail volumes rose 1.2% in May and 0.4% over three months. Retailers cited promotions and hot weather, while non-store volumes jumped 6.1% in the month. [ONS retail sales, 19 June]

My view

The retail rebound shows that April’s GDP fall did not become an immediate consumer retreat. Its dependence on promotions and weather matters: it may reveal price-sensitive demand rather than a durable rise in purchasing power. I would look for volumes to hold after discounts fade before upgrading the household outlook.

China

Private capex contracts

Equipment purchases rise while private investment falls

Retail sales fell 0.6% from a year earlier in May, though sales excluding autos rose 1.1%. Industrial value added grew 4.5%, up from 4.1% in April. [NBS retail sales] [NBS industrial production]

Fixed-asset investment fell 4.1% in the first five months and private investment fell 7.1%. Equipment purchases rose 9.3%, while manufacturing investment slipped 0.4%. [NBS fixed-asset investment, 17 June]

My view

Autos explain the negative retail headline, but they are not the only source of caution. Private investment was contracting even as equipment purchases rose, which suggests directed upgrading inside a weaker appetite to commit capital broadly. The next question is whether private investment stabilises; another equipment gain would not answer it.

What to watch

Four tests after the forecast downgrade

US housing pipeline

Permits held steadier than starts and the authorised-but-not-started backlog rose. Project conversion will show whether May was volatility or delayed construction.

Energy transmission

Transport costs, services prices and wage settlements will show whether the commodity shock becomes domestic inflation.

UK promotion sensitivity

Discounts and warm weather lifted May sales. Volumes after those effects fade will be a cleaner test of purchasing power.

China private capital

Equipment purchases increased while private investment contracted. New private projects would matter more than further spending on existing upgrade plans.
