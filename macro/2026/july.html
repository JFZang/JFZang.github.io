Monthly Macro Highlights

The recovery is holding,
but the hand-off is uneven

Inflation improved in the US and UK, yet growth still leaned on a narrow set of engines. China’s mid-year figures told a similar story from the other side: strong production, restrained consumption.

July 2026Data through 24 July 2026By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

By late July, the world economy looked steadier than it had in the spring. But the improvement was not broad enough to feel comfortable. Technology investment was carrying more of global demand, US hiring had slowed, UK growth was concentrated in services, and Chinese consumers remained careful.

Global

Resilience, with a narrower base

The IMF’s July update forecast global growth of 3.0% in 2026 and 3.4% in 2027. It described two opposing forces: an energy shock linked to the conflict in the Middle East, and stronger investment associated with AI and the wider technology cycle. The result was a world economy that was still expanding, but with very different outcomes for energy importers, exporters and technology producers. [IMF, 8 July]

The same update raised the global inflation forecast and said disinflation had stalled. That did not mean every country faced the same price problem. It did mean that a quick, coordinated round of rate cuts looked less plausible than it had at the start of the year.

My view

I see a slowdown as more likely than a recession. The concern is the narrowness of the support: technology spending could remain strong without lifting the rest of the economy evenly. I therefore prefer companies already converting investment demand into revenue and cash flow, rather than treating the AI cycle as a reason to lift every related valuation.

Markets and policy

July’s data pull rates and earnings in opposite directions

June payroll growth slowed to 57,000 and earlier months were revised down, while headline CPI fell 0.4% and core prices were unchanged. Those releases strengthen the case for lower interest rates. They also warn that company revenue may soften if weaker hiring reaches household spending. [BLS, 2 July] [BLS, 14 July]

This is why a fall in bond yields is not automatically positive for equities. Lower yields lift the present value of future profits, but that benefit can be offset if the profits themselves are revised down. I would want steadier hiring and broader revenue growth before treating easier financial conditions as an all-clear signal.

United States

Cooling, not contracting

Better inflation data arrived alongside softer hiring

The June employment report showed payrolls rising by 57,000 and unemployment at 4.2%. Participation fell to 61.5%, while April and May payrolls were revised down by a combined 74,000. Wage growth remained positive at 3.5% year on year. [BLS, 2 July]

Inflation offered more encouragement. Headline CPI fell 0.4% in June, mainly because of energy, and core prices were unchanged on the month. Over twelve months, headline inflation was 3.5% and core inflation 2.6%. Shelter rose only 0.1% in June, its smallest monthly increase since January 2021. [BLS, 14 July]

My view

The combination looks consistent with a soft landing, but it is too early to declare victory. Energy explains much of the monthly fall in prices, and one good core reading does not settle the inflation question. The labour figures also deserve more attention than the unemployment rate alone: fewer new jobs and lower participation suggest that demand for workers is losing strength.

United Kingdom

Services-led progress

Growth continued, though it was far from broad-based

GDP grew 0.1% in May after falling 0.1% in April. Across the three months to May, output rose 0.7%, led by a 0.7% increase in services. Construction grew over that period, but fell sharply in May, while monthly production also declined. [ONS, 16 July]

June CPI inflation fell to 2.6% from 2.8% in May. The direction was welcome, though inflation was still above the 2% target and the energy backdrop remained uncertain. [ONS, 22 July]

My view

I take the UK data as gradual improvement rather than a strong upswing. Services are doing most of the work, which leaves the recovery exposed to household incomes and financing costs. Lower inflation helps, but I do not think it justifies assuming a rapid fall in interest rates. For UK-focused businesses, volume growth and debt maturity schedules remain more useful than the headline GDP number.

China

Production ahead of demand

Mid-year data exposed the gap inside the economy

China’s preliminary estimate put second-quarter GDP growth at 4.3% year on year and first-half growth at 4.7%. Services grew faster than the secondary sector, while construction contracted. [NBS, 17 July]

June industrial value added rose 5.3% year on year, including 14.1% growth in high-technology manufacturing. New-energy vehicle output increased 29.4%. [NBS industrial production] Retail sales, however, grew only 1.0%, or 3.0% excluding autos. [NBS retail sales] CPI was up 1.0% from a year earlier and down 0.3% on the month. [NBS CPI]

My view

The production numbers show real strength in advanced manufacturing. The weaker retail figures show why that strength has not yet become a balanced recovery. The more durable investment cases are in businesses able to gain export share or improve factory productivity. A broad consumer call still depends on confidence and property conditions improving, neither of which the June data prove.

What I am watching

Questions for the weeks ahead

US policy

The key is whether softer hiring is confirmed by hours, claims and income without renewed core inflation. That combination would give the Fed room to ease for the right reason.

UK rates

Lower June inflation helps, but energy pass-through is still uncertain. Services prices and wage settlements will decide whether the dissent for higher rates fades.

China consumption

Household support matters only if it narrows the gap between factory output and retail demand. I am watching breadth, not simply subsidised categories.

Technology breadth

AI investment is a genuine demand engine, but concentration raises risk. Productivity and orders outside the immediate supply chain would make the cycle more durable.
