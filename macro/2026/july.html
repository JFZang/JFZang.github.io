Monthly Macro Highlights

July 2026 Macro Highlights

Lower inflation gave households some relief, but spending did not rise broadly. US hiring slowed, UK retail gains depended partly on promotions and warm weather, and Chinese households saved more of their income growth.

July 2026Reporting window: 26 June–24 July 2026By Jiefu Zhang

Central question

Why did lower inflation not immediately restore household spending?

Spending did improve in some areas, but not across the whole economy. US households paid less for energy while hiring slowed. UK retail sales rose mainly in goods affected by promotions and warm weather, while mortgage costs increased. Chinese household income grew faster than spending, especially in cities. Lower prices helped with current bills, but they did not remove worries about jobs, borrowing or housing wealth.

What changed this month

Lower prices helped, but spending rose only in selected areas

US CPI: −0.4% m/m

June energy prices fell 5.7% and gasoline 9.7%; core prices were unchanged.

UK retail volumes: +1.0% m/m

June sales rose, led by promotions, warm weather, clothing, outdoor goods and online shopping.

UK mortgage approvals: 56,200

May approvals fell from 66,000 as the effective rate on new mortgages rose to 4.22%.

China H1 real spending: +2.7% y/y

Household spending lagged the 4.2% increase in real disposable income.

Evidence and mechanism

Cheaper goods could not replace stable income or affordable borrowing

US households received the clearest relief from cheaper energy. June CPI fell 0.4%, the largest monthly decline since April 2020, as energy fell 5.7% and gasoline 9.7%; core prices were unchanged. The employment report was less reassuring. Payrolls rose by 57,000, participation fell 0.3 percentage point to 61.5%, and the number unemployed for 27 weeks or longer was 286,000 higher than a year earlier. Lower fuel bills left more money available to spend, but new labour income was growing more slowly. [BLS CPI, 14 July] [BLS employment, 2 July]

The UK data were not entirely weak. Retail volumes rose 1.0% in June and 0.6% over the quarter. However, the ONS linked much of the monthly increase to promotions and warm weather, including clothing, outdoor goods and fans. Fuel volumes remained below February after March stockpiling and the later reversal. Households responded to bargains and specific needs, but the figures did not yet show a general willingness to spend more. [ONS retail sales, 24 July]

Financing conditions explain part of the gap. UK mortgage approvals for house purchase fell from 66,000 in April to 56,200 in May, while the effective rate on newly drawn mortgages rose from 4.08% to 4.22%. Households added GBP 5.4 billion to deposits, including GBP 3.1 billion to individual savings accounts, even as consumer credit grew. Some of the inflation relief was therefore being saved or offset by a more expensive housing decision rather than spent across the high street. [Bank of England, 29 June]

China added a third explanation. First-half real disposable income rose 4.2%, but real consumption expenditure increased 2.7%. Urban real consumption rose only 2.0%, urban property income fell 0.1% in nominal terms, and median household income grew more slowly than the mean. Lower inflation alone could not solve this. When property income is weak and the typical household earns less than the average suggests, a rise in real income does not have to produce the same rise in spending. [NBS household survey, 16 July]

Answer

Lower inflation helped households, but it was not enough on its own. Spending rose where discounts, lower prices or warm weather gave people an immediate reason to buy. A broader improvement still required steadier US labour income, cheaper UK mortgages and more confidence among Chinese urban households. Until then, part of the saving from lower prices was likely to go towards debt payments or cash reserves.

United States

Cheaper fuel, slower hiring

Cheaper energy helped households while hiring slowed

June's 0.4% fall in CPI was dominated by energy, while core prices were flat and core inflation eased to 2.6% over the year. Advance retail data showed that sales were little changed after a much stronger May. The absence of a broad sales surge is less surprising when set beside payroll growth of 57,000 and lower participation.

One monthly employment report does not establish a downturn. It does show why the inflation gain may have been saved rather than spent: a household responds to a lower petrol bill differently when hours, job switching or future wage income feel less secure.

My view

I was more interested in total real labour income than in CPI alone. If hours worked and payrolls stabilised while energy stayed cheaper, households would have more reason to spend beyond essential or discounted goods. If employment income kept weakening, lower inflation would reduce the damage but probably would not create a broad spending recovery.

United Kingdom

Retail up, mortgages down

Promotions lifted retail sales while mortgage approvals fell

June retail volumes rose 1.0%, and the three-month measure increased 0.6%. Non-store retail, clothing and household goods benefited from promotions and warm weather. This was a real improvement, not merely higher prices, but its composition was unusually event-sensitive.

Housing finance moved the other way. House-purchase approvals fell to 56,200 and the rate on new mortgages rose to 4.22%. At the same time, households increased deposits and could earn 4.26% on new time deposits. Spending therefore competed with both higher financing costs and a more attractive return on cash.

My view

The June retail increase showed that households did respond to better prices and good weather. The question was whether spending would remain stronger after the promotions and warm weather ended. Another broad rise in retail volumes, together with more mortgage approvals, would make the improvement look more lasting.

China

Income grew faster than spending

Urban households saved more as property income weakened

Real disposable income per person rose 4.2% in the first half, compared with 2.7% growth in real consumption. Urban real income increased 3.4% and consumption 2.0%; rural measures grew 5.5% and 3.8%. Nominal property income rose only 1.1% nationally and fell 0.1% in urban areas. The median income gain of 4.7% also trailed the 5.2% increase in the mean.

The spending mix was not uniformly weak. Outlays on household facilities and services rose 5.3%, and education, culture and recreation increased 4.9%, while residence spending grew 1.4%. Separately, goods retail sales rose 1.1% in the first half, catering 2.8%, and online retail of goods and services 5.2%. [NBS retail sales, 16 July]

My view

The national average made household finances look stronger than they were for the typical urban family. Weak property income and spending on housing helped explain why more of the income gain was saved. I would find an improvement in urban property income and median household income more convincing than another national retail target.

Underfollowed indicator

Lower inflation can lead to spending, saving or debt repayment

Households can spend extra real income, save it or use it to repay debt. UK deposits rose while mortgage approvals fell, Chinese income grew faster than consumption, and US energy bills fell as hiring slowed. These choices help show whether lower inflation is only making current bills easier to pay or is actually making households more willing to spend.

Since last month

What continued

What changed

Oil risk

What would change my view

Wider retail gains, stable employment, more mortgage approvals and faster urban spending would show a broader recovery.
