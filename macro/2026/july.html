Monthly Macro Highlights

US inflation fell but hiring and retail sales slowed, UK mortgage approvals dropped, and Chinese rural income and consumption grew faster than urban measures.

July 2026Reporting window: 26 June–24 July 2026By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

July shifts from June’s forecast downgrade to household transmission. US spending lost speed, UK mortgage approvals fell despite lower inflation, and Chinese urban consumption grew more slowly than rural consumption while property income barely increased.

Global

Lower inflation does not immediately restore household demand

US headline CPI fell 0.4% in June and UK inflation eased to 2.6%. Yet US retail sales rose only 0.2% after a 0.9% May gain, while UK mortgage approvals fell from 66,000 in April to 56,200 in May and the effective rate on new mortgages rose from 4.08% to 4.22%. [BLS, 14 July] [ONS, 22 July] [US Census, 16 July] [Bank of England, 29 June]

Chinese household data showed a similar lag through a different channel. Real disposable income rose 4.2% in the first half, but real consumption expenditure increased 2.7%; urban consumption grew only 2.0%. [NBS household survey, 17 July]

My view

July’s surprise is not that inflation fell; it is that the demand channels did not improve with it. Mortgage approvals and US retail momentum remained soft, while Chinese households spent less quickly than their incomes grew. Lower inflation helps cash flow, but these releases do not show a renewed appetite for debt or discretionary spending.

Policy and market implications

Lower yields help valuations only if spending holds

US payroll growth slowed to 57,000, earlier months were revised down and June headline CPI fell 0.4%. Those releases reduce one barrier to lower interest rates but also raise a revenue question if weaker hiring reaches spending. [BLS employment, 2 July] [BLS CPI, 14 July]

UK credit data show the transmission more directly. Mortgage approvals fell from 66,000 in April to 56,200 in May, while the effective rate on new mortgages rose from 4.08% to 4.22%. Consumer-credit growth nevertheless remained 8.9% year on year. [Bank of England credit data, 29 June]

My view

A fall in bond yields can raise equity valuations, but it is not sufficient evidence of stronger demand if households are finding mortgages harder to obtain or companies expect softer revenue. I would want lower market rates to appear in approvals and refinancing costs before treating them as genuine support.

United States

Disinflation, less momentum

Inflation falls as hiring and retail sales lose speed

June payrolls rose by 57,000, unemployment was 4.2% and participation fell to 61.5%. Headline CPI fell 0.4% in the month, core prices were unchanged, and shelter recorded its smallest monthly rise since January 2021. [BLS employment, 2 July] [BLS CPI, 14 July]

Retail and food-service sales rose only 0.2% in June after a 0.9% May gain, though they remained 6.7% higher than a year earlier. [US Census, 16 July]

My view

The fall in inflation came with slower hiring, participation and monthly retail growth. That combination can improve real purchasing power while weakening the income needed to use it. Annual sales growth remains positive, but another payroll revision or a fall in real retail volumes would show that June’s disinflation was arriving through weaker demand rather than cheaper supply.

United Kingdom

Services up, mortgages down

Services grow as mortgage approvals fall

GDP grew 0.1% in May and 0.7% over the latest three months, led by services. June CPI inflation fell to 2.6%. [ONS GDP, 16 July] [ONS CPI, 22 July]

Mortgage approvals fell to 56,200 in May from 66,000, and the effective rate on new mortgages rose to 4.22%. At the same time, annual consumer-credit growth was 8.9%. [Bank of England, 29 June]

My view

Services are growing, but the household financing channel is not improving in the same way. Fewer mortgage approvals and a higher new-mortgage rate restrain housing turnover and financed purchases, while strong consumer-credit growth may reflect borrowing for current spending rather than confidence. That makes the recovery more credit-sensitive than the GDP headline suggests.

China

Urban spending lags

Rural incomes and consumption grow faster than urban measures

Real per-capita disposable income rose 4.2% in the first half of 2026. Urban real income grew 3.4%, compared with 5.5% in rural areas; the national median increased 4.7% in nominal terms, compared with a 5.2% rise in the mean. [NBS household survey, 17 July]

Real consumption expenditure rose 2.7%, including 2.0% in urban areas and 3.8% in rural areas. Property income increased only 1.1%; spending on housing rose 1.4%, compared with 4.9% for education, culture and recreation and 5.3% for household facilities and services. [NBS household survey, 17 July]

My view

The urban-rural split matters more this month than another comparison between factories and retail sales. Rural income and consumption grew faster from lower levels, while urban real spending rose only 2.0% and property income barely increased. The weakness is concentrated in urban balance sheets and housing-linked income rather than shared equally across households.

What to watch

Four questions raised by the household data

US revisions and real sales

Hiring and monthly retail growth both slowed. Payroll revisions and inflation-adjusted sales will show whether income or purchasing power is the main issue.

UK mortgage approvals

Approvals fell while new mortgage rates rose. Housing transactions and subsequent approvals will show whether this is a brief financing interruption.

China urban spending

Urban consumption and property income lagged rural measures. Housing-linked income and discretionary services will show whether that gap persists.

Company revenue

Lower inflation supports real purchasing power only if income and credit hold. Consumer-facing revenue guidance will show whether firms are seeing that benefit.
