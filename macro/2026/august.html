Monthly Macro Highlights

Resilient growth,
less comfortable inflation

The global economy is holding up, but the mix is becoming harder for central banks: technology investment supports demand while energy risks and regional imbalances complicate disinflation.

August 2026 Data cut-off: 21 August 2026 By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

August's releases do not tell one neat global story. US hiring has lost momentum, the UK remains sensitive to energy costs, and Chinese consumers are still cautious. At the same time, investment in technology and parts of manufacturing remain firm. I think that makes company and sector choice more useful than trying to label the whole backdrop “risk-on” or “risk-off”.

Global

Two forces are pulling in opposite directions

The IMF’s July update puts global growth at 3.0% in 2026 and 3.4% in 2027. Beneath those figures, AI-related investment is helping economies tied to the technology supply chain, while the energy shock from the Middle East is hurting importers most. The IMF also says the fall in global inflation has stalled. [IMF, July 2026]

So the growth forecast looks steadier than the underlying picture. Technology spending can support orders and profits, but dearer energy takes money away from households and raises business costs. That leaves central banks with an awkward trade-off: less protection against a slowdown, but no clear licence to cut rates quickly.

My view

I still see continued, modest expansion as more likely than a global recession. But the gaps between countries and industries should widen. I would rather rely on durable earnings, sound balance sheets and pricing power than on the hope of a broad fall in discount rates. A wider spread of productivity gains beyond technology would improve this view. Another energy spike feeding into wages would weaken it.

Markets and policy

Resilience is keeping the policy hedge expensive

The Federal Reserve held its target range at 3.5–3.75% in July, while the Bank of England maintained Bank Rate at 3.75%. The shared decision masks different concerns: softer US employment sits beside sticky inflation, while the UK remains exposed to energy pass-through. [Federal Reserve, 29 July] [Bank of England, 30 July]

Gold, oil, government bonds and volatility can all respond to this backdrop for different reasons. Rather than treating one asset as a definitive macro signal, I am looking for confirmation: defensive assets rising alongside wider credit spreads would say something more troubling than gold strength on its own.

United States

Late-cycle tension

The jobs data have weakened, but inflation has not gone away

July nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000 and the unemployment rate was 4.1%. Revisions also reduced May and June payroll growth by a combined 103,000. [BLS employment report] At the same time, July CPI rose 3.4% from a year earlier, while core CPI was 2.5%. Energy inflation remained high year on year even though energy prices fell during July. [BLS CPI report]

The Federal Reserve held the federal funds target range at 3.50–3.75% in July. Notably, three voters preferred a 25 basis-point increase. [Federal Reserve, 29 July] The split is revealing: the employment figures are softer, but several policymakers are still more worried about inflation than growth.

My view

One poor payroll report does not make a recession, though it does show that high rates are reaching the labour market. My base case is slower US growth and a Fed that waits for clearer evidence on services inflation before changing course. In equities, I would favour businesses where productivity is already visible in cash flow, and be more careful with highly leveraged companies whose case rests mainly on cheaper funding.

United Kingdom

Measured caution

Growth is positive, but the policy margin is narrow

UK real GDP grew by 0.4% in the second quarter, after 0.6% growth in the first. Services remained the main engine, while production was flat across the three months to June. June GDP itself rose 0.3%, helped by a 0.4% increase in services. [ONS, June 2026 GDP]

The Bank of England kept Bank Rate at 3.75% at its July meeting, with three MPC members preferring an increase to 4.0%. CPI inflation had fallen to 2.6%, but the Bank expects higher energy costs to lift inflation later in the year and is watching for second-round effects through wages and prices. [Bank of England, July 2026]

My view

The UK is growing, although much of that growth still comes from services and could be knocked by another squeeze on real incomes. Even if rate cuts begin, I do not expect an immediate rebound. Services inflation is still elevated enough to keep the Bank of England cautious, and lower policy rates take time to reach mortgages and business loans. For UK-facing companies, refinancing needs and fixed costs therefore matter as much as the GDP headline. Businesses earning abroad are less dependent on a strong domestic consumer.

China

Supply-led growth

Factories are doing better than households

July industrial value added grew 4.5% year on year, with high-technology manufacturing up 16.9%. Electronics production grew strongly and new-energy vehicle output rose 29.9%. [NBS industrial production] Yet the manufacturing PMI slipped to 49.2, below the 50 line. [NBS manufacturing PMI]

Household demand was much softer. July retail sales rose only 0.6% year on year, or 2.5% excluding autos. [NBS retail sales] Headline CPI increased 0.5%. [NBS CPI] In other words, China’s factories remain competitive, but consumers are not yet matching their pace.

My view

China’s strength in advanced manufacturing is real, but adding supply is not the same as creating balanced demand. A durable pickup needs stronger household confidence and income, as well as less drag from property. I would separate exporters and automation businesses that can win share on their own merits from companies that need a quick, broad consumer recovery to make the numbers work.

What I am watching

What would change the autumn view

Energy pass-through

Lower monthly energy prices are helpful only if they persist. Transport costs, wage plans and services prices will show whether the earlier shock has become embedded.

US labour breadth

Weakness in a few sectors can reverse; falling hours, claims and income together would be harder to dismiss and would threaten consumption.

UK services inflation

Domestic services remain the policy constraint. A sustained decline would give the Bank room to ease without relying on lower energy alone.

China demand mix

I want policy support to reach household consumption rather than add mainly to industrial supply. Retail breadth and property activity are the practical test.
