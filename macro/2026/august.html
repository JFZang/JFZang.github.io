Monthly Macro Highlights

August 2026 Macro Highlights

US payrolls weakened in July, while second-quarter listed-company results beat expectations across most sectors. Two large investment gains exaggerated the headline profit number, so revenue growth and the adjusted earnings surprise gave a better measure of business momentum. Elsewhere, UK business investment rose, UK energy inflation returned and China's equipment spending remained stronger than total private investment.

August 2026Reporting window: 25 July–21 August 2026By Jiefu Zhang

Central question

How much of the 50.4% rise in S&P 500 earnings came from normal business performance?

Two unusually large investment gains made the headline much stronger than the underlying results. Removing Alphabet and Amazon reduced blended earnings growth from 50.4% to 32.0%. That was still a strong quarter: revenue grew 15.0%, all eleven sectors reported revenue growth and the earnings surprise remained above its historical average after the same adjustment. Ordinary profits had not risen by half; the quarter was genuinely strong, but the headline made the improvement look larger than it was.

What changed this month

The month added an earnings-quality question to weaker employment data

US payrolls: −23,000

July employment fell, and May–June payroll gains were revised down by a combined 103,000.

Blended S&P revenue: +15.0% y/y

With 88% of companies reporting, 76% had beaten revenue estimates and every sector reported sales growth.

Provisional UK business investment: +1.7% q/q

Capital spending improved even as quarterly GDP growth slowed and consumer inflation rose.

China Jan–Jul private investment: −9.4% y/y

Private fixed investment fell while equipment purchases increased 9.0%.

Evidence and mechanism

Two investment gains inflated the headline, but did not create the whole improvement

The 50.4% figure combined normal company results with two unusually large gains. Alphabet recorded USD 98 billion of unrealised gains on equity securities, while Amazon recorded USD 53.4 billion of other income mainly related to its Anthropic investment. These gains did not come from selling more goods or services. Removing both companies reduced blended earnings growth to 32.0% and the aggregate earnings surprise from 29.2% to 10.9%. [FactSet, 7 August]

The adjusted figures were still strong. With 88% of the index reporting, 76% of companies had beaten revenue estimates, aggregate revenue was 3.2% above estimates and year-on-year revenue growth reached 15.0%. All eleven sectors reported revenue growth and ten reported earnings growth. The improvement was therefore broader than Alphabet and Amazon, even though the headline overstated its size.

The payroll report covered a later period. US employment fell by 23,000 in July, the three-month average slowed to 20,000, and the May and June estimates were revised down by a combined 103,000. Second-quarter earnings mainly described activity from April to June, so the two releases did not directly contradict each other. The labour data were a warning for the next quarter rather than an explanation of the previous one. [BLS employment, 7 August]

Financing was not yet an immediate constraint. Banks left business-lending standards broadly unchanged, reported stronger demand from large and medium-sized firms, and reduced some loan spreads and credit-line costs. This did not guarantee another strong quarter, but large firms were not yet facing an abrupt financing squeeze. [Federal Reserve SLOOS, 3 August]

The valuation data pointed in the same direction. FactSet's forward 12-month price-to-earnings ratio was 20.0 on 7 August, below 20.4 at the end of June, while its estimate of second-quarter earnings had risen over the same period. The market was still expensive, but the stronger index was not based only on investors accepting a higher multiple.

Answer

The headline overstated normal business performance because index earnings include investment and other income, and two very large companies recorded exceptional gains at the same time. Revenue was not affected by those gains, so its broad increase showed that the quarter was not only an accounting effect. I would use the 32.0% adjusted growth rate as the better starting point and then check whether third-quarter sales and margin estimates survived the weaker July employment report.

Market dashboard

Earnings estimates, rather than multiple expansion, did more of the work

S&P 500: +3.5%

The index rose from 7,413.18 on 27 July, the first trading day in the window, to 7,674.37 on 21 August.

High-yield spread: −11bp

The spread narrowed from 2.81% on 27 July to 2.70% on 21 August; bond investors did not price a near-term default wave.

VIX: 15.13

Equity volatility remained subdued on 21 August despite the payroll revisions.

Forward P/E: 20.0

The multiple remained elevated but below its 20.4 reading at the end of June.

The S&P 500 rose while the latest forward price-to-earnings ratio remained below its end-June level. FactSet also reported that its second-quarter earnings estimate had increased as companies released results. That made earnings revisions a better explanation for the move than another expansion in the valuation multiple. The narrower high-yield spread and low VIX showed that investors were not adding a large risk discount at the same time. [FRED, S&P 500] [FRED, high-yield spread] [Cboe, VIX]

Policy and markets

Policy remains tight, but markets are not yet pricing a downturn

The Federal Reserve held its target range at 3.50–3.75% on 29 July, and the Bank of England kept Bank Rate at 3.75% the next day. Neither statement promised a near-term cut. The US then reported weaker employment alongside July CPI inflation of 3.4%, while UK July inflation rose to 2.9% as energy pressure returned. [Federal Reserve, 29 July] [Bank of England, 30 July] [BLS CPI, 12 August]

My view

One poor payroll report does not make a recession, though it does show that high rates are reaching the labour market. This mattered because the strong earnings release described the previous quarter. Company guidance and forecast revisions would show whether July's weaker hiring was an early sign of slower sales or mainly a change in labour demand.

United States

Strong profits, weaker jobs

Company results were strong, but they described the previous quarter

Part of the difference came from timing. Second-quarter earnings covered sales and profits earned before the July fall in payrolls, while the employment report described more recent labour demand. The two measures also covered different things. Listed companies could increase profits through productivity, overseas sales and lower costs without hiring the same number of US workers.

July CPI rose 0.1% and energy fell 1.5%; core prices increased 0.2%. Inflation was not reaccelerating sharply, but annual headline inflation of 3.4% left the Federal Reserve with less freedom than a weak payroll number alone would suggest. [BLS CPI, 12 August]

My view

The next question was whether companies were hiring less because productivity had improved or because they expected weaker demand. Stable revenue guidance would support the first explanation; cuts to sales forecasts would support the second. The next payroll revisions and company guidance were therefore more useful than trying to label the whole cycle from one month of data.

United Kingdom

Investment up, energy costs up

Business investment improved while household energy costs rose

UK GDP grew 0.4% in the second quarter after 0.6% in the first, while the provisional business-investment estimate increased 1.7% in the quarter and 0.8% over the year. Companies were still committing capital even as the overall pace slowed, but the investment estimate could still be revised. [ONS GDP, 13 August] [ONS business investment, 13 August]

July consumer inflation rose to 2.9% from 2.6%, with energy costs adding pressure. Higher fuel and utility bills reduce the income available for other purchases, while the Bank cannot offset the squeeze quickly if it fears that firms and workers will reset prices and pay. The decision to hold Bank Rate did not imply that a reduction was imminent. [ONS CPI, 19 August]

My view

UK business investment was more encouraging than the inflation figure, but it did not protect households from higher energy bills. I wanted to see whether firms kept investing if consumer-facing sales weakened. That would tell us more than trying to guess when the Bank might lower rates.

China

Equipment stronger than total investment

Equipment purchases rose while overall investment fell

Fixed-asset investment fell 6.7% in January–July and private investment 9.4%. Manufacturing investment declined 1.7%, but equipment purchases rose 9.0% and electronics investment 7.8%. July's manufacturing PMI stood at 49.2, below the line separating expansion from contraction. [NBS fixed investment, 18 August] [NBS PMI, 3 August]

This was not a general rise in capital spending. Firms involved in equipment replacement and electronics could expand while private projects elsewhere were cancelled or delayed. Selected suppliers and listed manufacturers could benefit even though investment across the wider economy continued to fall.

My view

The main question was how far the equipment-upgrade programme had spread. If private investment and new project starts also rose, the increase in equipment demand would look broader. If equipment purchases kept growing while total investment fell, the improvement would remain limited to a smaller group of firms.

Underfollowed indicator

The revenue surprise provided a cleaner check on earnings quality

Investment gains can increase earnings without adding to sales. Revenue therefore provided a separate check: 76% of companies beat revenue estimates, aggregate revenue was 3.2% above forecasts and every sector reported year-on-year growth. This did not reveal how much came from prices rather than volumes, but it showed that the earnings improvement was supported by normal business activity outside the two exceptional gains.

What would change my view
