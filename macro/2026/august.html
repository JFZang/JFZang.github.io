Monthly Macro Highlights

Growth became less balanced across the globe: US hiring weakened, UK energy costs sustained price pressure, and China’s industrial output continued to outpace household spending.

August 2026 Reporting window: 25 July–21 August 2026 By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

August shows a wider split between company output and household purchasing power. US productivity rose while real hourly compensation fell, UK business investment improved as consumer prices rose, and Chinese manufacturing investment contracted despite faster high-technology output. Equities and credit spreads remained calm.

Global

Productivity and capital spending diverge across countries

US nonfarm productivity rose at a 1.4% annualised rate in the second quarter as output grew faster than hours worked. UK business investment increased 1.7%. Chinese manufacturing investment moved the other way, falling 1.7%, even as equipment purchases rose 9.0%. [BLS productivity, 6 August] [ONS business investment, 13 August] [NBS fixed-asset investment, 18 August]

The three figures describe different company responses. US firms produced more per hour, UK firms added capital spending, and Chinese firms concentrated purchases in equipment while reducing manufacturing investment overall. None of those changes can be inferred from the weaker employment, inflation or retail headlines alone.

My view

US productivity gives firms a cost cushion, UK capital spending adds future capacity, and China’s equipment purchases look more like selected upgrades than sector-wide expansion. The immediate risk is therefore concentrated in household-facing revenue and Chinese manufacturers outside the upgrade cycle, rather than in all company output.

Policy and market implications

Policy remains tight, but markets are not yet pricing a downturn

The Federal Reserve held its target range at 3.5–3.75% in July, while the Bank of England maintained Bank Rate at 3.75%. Softer US employment sat beside sticky inflation, while the UK remained exposed to energy pass-through. [Federal Reserve, 29 July] [Bank of England, 30 July]

The S&P 500 rose about 3.5% during the reporting window, the US high-yield spread moved only from 2.85 percentage points at the end of July to 2.94 on 20 August, and the VIX closed at 15.13 on 21 August. Gold alone is ambiguous; the absence of wider spreads and higher volatility is the more useful confirmation check. [FRED, S&P 500] [FRED, high-yield spread] [Cboe, VIX]

My view

Equities, high-yield spreads and the VIX imply that investors assign little probability to a sharp earnings or default cycle. Those prices mainly reflect large listed borrowers, so they can coexist with weaker household credit. A joint rise in spreads and volatility would be the point at which market pricing begins to confirm the demand data.

United States

Productivity up, real pay down

Productivity rises while real hourly compensation falls

Nonfarm business productivity increased at a 1.4% annualised rate in the second quarter as output rose 1.7% and hours worked 0.3%. Unit labour costs increased 1.3%; from a year earlier, productivity rose 2.2% and unit labour costs 1.4%. [BLS productivity, 6 August]

Real hourly compensation fell at a 3.1% annualised rate in the quarter and was 0.1% lower than a year earlier. Manufacturing productivity rose 1.9%, leaving manufacturing unit labour costs unchanged during the quarter. [BLS productivity, 6 August]

My view

Higher productivity and flat manufacturing unit labour costs give firms more room to absorb weaker sales without cutting output immediately. Real hourly compensation moved the other way, so the same quarter offered little relief to household purchasing power. That combination is consistent with factory activity holding up while retail demand softens.

United Kingdom

Capex offsets slower growth

Business investment rises despite slower GDP

Real GDP grew 0.4% in the second quarter after 0.6% in the first. Services remained the main contributor, while production was flat across the three months to June. July CPI inflation rose from 2.6% to 2.9% as higher energy costs reached consumers. [ONS, June GDP] [ONS, July CPI]

Business investment increased 1.7% from the first quarter and 0.8% from a year earlier. [ONS business investment, 13 August]

My view

The 1.7% investment rise is large enough to qualify the slower GDP reading, but it does not show whether firms added capacity or brought projects forward. Higher consumer prices affect households now; investment affects output with a lag. The next capex estimate and new orders will show which side of that timing difference lasts.

China

Investment breadth weakens

Equipment spending rises inside a broader investment contraction

July industrial value added grew 4.5% year on year, down from 5.3% in June, while high-technology manufacturing rose 16.9%. The manufacturing PMI was 49.2. Retail sales rose 0.6%, or 2.5% excluding autos. [NBS industrial production] [NBS manufacturing PMI] [NBS retail sales]

Fixed-asset investment fell 6.7% in the first seven months and non-governmental investment fell 9.4%. Manufacturing investment was down 1.7%, yet equipment purchases rose 9.0% and electronics investment 7.8%. [NBS fixed-asset investment, 18 August]

My view

July showed households spending cautiously; August moves to the corporate response. Manufacturing investment fell even though equipment purchases and electronics investment rose, which suggests firms were upgrading selected production lines rather than expanding capacity across the sector. The sub-50 PMI makes that distinction harder to dismiss as project timing alone.

What to watch

What would change the autumn view

US productivity and pay

Output rose faster than hours while real hourly compensation fell. The next revision and real-income data will show whether the gain reaches households.

UK investment follow-through

Business investment rose despite slower GDP. The next estimate and new orders will show whether firms added capacity or only brought projects forward.

China investment breadth

Equipment purchases grew while manufacturing investment fell. New private projects would indicate expansion beyond selected production upgrades.

Market confirmation

Equities, volatility and credit spreads remained calm. A joint rise in spreads and the VIX would show that weaker demand had reached default and earnings expectations.
