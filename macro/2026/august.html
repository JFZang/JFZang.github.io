Monthly Macro Highlights

Resilient growth,
less comfortable inflation

The global economy is holding up, but the mix is becoming harder for central banks: technology investment supports demand while energy risks and regional imbalances complicate disinflation.

August 2026 Reporting window: 25 July–21 August 2026 By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

August's releases do not tell one neat global story. US hiring has lost momentum, the UK remains sensitive to energy costs, and Chinese consumers are still cautious. At the same time, investment in technology and parts of manufacturing remain firm. That makes company and sector choice more useful than trying to label the whole backdrop “risk-on” or “risk-off”.

Global

August adds a clearer warning on demand

The July issue established a world economy still expanding through technology investment despite higher energy costs. August’s releases shift the balance towards weaker demand. US payrolls fell by 23,000, including downward revisions to May and June; Chinese industrial output grew 4.5%, but retail sales rose only 0.6%; and UK growth slowed from 0.6% in the first quarter to 0.4% in the second. [BLS employment report] [NBS industrial production] [NBS retail sales] [ONS, June GDP]

Energy has not disappeared as a constraint. At its July meeting, the Bank of England recorded Brent crude at $84 a barrel and UK front-month gas at 136 pence per therm, both materially above pre-conflict levels. [Bank of England, July 2026] The new problem is that this cost pressure now sits beside softer hiring and consumption. Central banks have less room to support growth because cutting too quickly could reinforce the remaining inflation.

My view

My central case is now a very weak expansion, with less cushion than I saw in July. Hiring and household demand are weakening before the energy constraint has fully faded, so companies cannot rely on either strong volume growth or rapid rate cuts. I would rather rely on durable earnings, sound balance sheets and pricing power. A recovery in hiring and retail demand would improve this view; another energy rise feeding into wages would weaken it.

Policy and market implications

Resilience is keeping the policy hedge expensive

The Federal Reserve held its target range at 3.5–3.75% in July, while the Bank of England maintained Bank Rate at 3.75%. The shared decision masks different concerns: softer US employment sits beside sticky inflation, while the UK remains exposed to energy pass-through. [Federal Reserve, 29 July] [Bank of England, 30 July]

Gold does not provide a clean answer on its own. It can rise because investors fear geopolitical disruption, but also because they expect lower real interest rates, a weaker dollar or continued central-bank demand. Oil is different again: a demand-led rise can accompany stronger activity, whereas a supply-led rise raises inflation while taking spending power away from consumers. Government bonds can rally on weaker growth, yet sell off if inflation or public borrowing is the larger concern.

This is why confirmation across markets matters more than assigning one meaning to one price. A rise in gold accompanied by materially wider corporate credit spreads and higher equity volatility would be more troubling because it would show that concern had reached companies’ funding costs and investors’ expectations of near-term risk. That confirmation was not present at the cut-off. The US high-yield option-adjusted spread moved only from 2.85 percentage points at the end of July to 2.94 on 20 August, while the VIX closed at 15.13 on 21 August, near the lower end of its 52-week range. [FRED, high-yield spread] [Cboe, VIX]

My view

I read the current pattern as selective hedging against uncertainty, not a wholesale flight from risk. Gold may be reflecting a genuine desire for protection, but calm volatility and broadly stable credit spreads suggest that investors are not yet pricing a severe rise in defaults or an abrupt contraction. I would become more defensive if volatility and spreads rose persistently alongside weaker cyclical equities. Until then, the message from cross-asset prices is caution rather than panic.

United States

Late-cycle tension

The jobs data have weakened, but inflation has not gone away

July nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000 and the unemployment rate was 4.1%. Revisions also reduced May and June payroll growth by a combined 103,000. [BLS employment report] At the same time, July CPI rose 3.4% from a year earlier, while core CPI was 2.5%. Energy inflation remained high year on year even though energy prices fell during July. [BLS CPI report]

The Federal Reserve held the federal funds target range at 3.50–3.75% in July. Notably, three voters preferred a 25 basis-point increase. [Federal Reserve, 29 July] The split is revealing: the employment figures are softer, but several policymakers are still more worried about inflation than growth.

My view

One poor payroll report does not make a recession, though it does show that high rates are reaching the labour market. My base case is slower US growth and a Fed that waits for clearer evidence on services inflation before changing course. In equities, I would favour businesses where productivity is already visible in cash flow, and be more careful with highly leveraged companies whose case rests mainly on cheaper funding.

United Kingdom

Measured caution

Growth is positive, but the policy margin is narrow

UK real GDP grew by 0.4% in the second quarter, after 0.6% growth in the first. Services remained the main engine, while production was flat across the three months to June. June GDP itself rose 0.3%, helped by a 0.4% increase in services. [ONS, June 2026 GDP]

The Bank of England kept Bank Rate at 3.75% at its July meeting, with three MPC members preferring an increase to 4.0%. The subsequent release showed CPI inflation rising from 2.6% in June to 2.9% in July as higher energy costs began to reach consumers. That does not prove that domestic inflation is accelerating, but it makes the Bank's concern about second-round effects through wages and prices less abstract. [Bank of England, July 2026] [ONS, July CPI]

My view

The UK is growing, but July's inflation rebound shows how easily an imported energy cost can erode that progress. Higher fuel and utility bills reduce the income available for other purchases, while the Bank cannot offset the squeeze quickly if it fears that firms and workers will reset prices and pay. I therefore do not expect an immediate consumer rebound even if rates eventually fall. For UK-facing companies, refinancing needs, fixed costs and the share of essential demand matter as much as the GDP headline. A renewed decline in services inflation would give the Bank more room and make the domestic outlook less fragile.

China

Supply-led growth

Factories are doing better than households

July industrial value added grew 4.5% year on year, with high-technology manufacturing up 16.9%. Electronics production grew strongly and new-energy vehicle output rose 29.9%. [NBS industrial production] Yet the manufacturing PMI slipped to 49.2, below the 50 line. [NBS manufacturing PMI]

Household demand was much softer. July retail sales rose only 0.6% year on year, or 2.5% excluding autos. [NBS retail sales] Headline CPI increased 0.5%. [NBS CPI] In other words, China’s factories remain competitive, but consumers are not yet matching their pace.

My view

China’s strength in advanced manufacturing is real, but adding supply is not the same as creating balanced demand. A durable pickup needs stronger household confidence and income, as well as less drag from property. I would separate exporters and automation businesses that can win share on their own merits from companies that need a quick, broad consumer recovery to make the numbers work.

What to watch

What would change the autumn view

Energy pass-through

Lower monthly energy prices are helpful only if they persist. Transport costs, wage plans and services prices will show whether the earlier shock has become embedded.

US labour breadth

Weakness in a few sectors can reverse; falling hours, claims and income together would be harder to dismiss and would threaten consumption.

UK services inflation

Domestic services remain the policy constraint. A sustained decline would give the Bank room to ease without relying on lower energy alone.

China demand mix

The test is whether policy support reaches household consumption rather than adding mainly to industrial supply. Retail breadth and property activity provide the practical evidence.
