Monthly Macro Highlights

US jobs and inflation data improved, but borrowing for homes and cars remained weak. Banks were more cautious with some borrowers, UK firms cut investment, and Chinese companies waited longer to be paid.

February 2026Reporting window: 24 January–20 February 2026By Jiefu Zhang

Central question

Why did better US jobs and inflation data not lead to more borrowing for homes and cars?

Payrolls improved and inflation slowed, but banks still reported weaker demand for mortgages, car loans and other consumer credit. They also expected more repayment problems among households and small businesses. Lower inflation helped with current bills, but borrowers still needed deposits and collateral, and both they and their banks remained uncertain about future income.

What changed this month

Lower inflation did not remove the difficulty of borrowing

US payrolls: +130,000

January hiring improved, but health care, social assistance and construction accounted for most of the increase.

US CPI: +2.4% y/y

Headline inflation slowed, helped by a 1.5% monthly energy decline; electricity and utility-gas prices remained much higher than a year earlier.

Household loan demand weakened

Banks reported less demand for most mortgages, auto loans and other consumer credit.

UK business investment: −2.7% q/q

The provisional fourth-quarter estimate fell despite remaining 2.0% above a year earlier.

Evidence and mechanism

Better monthly data did not make every borrower stronger

January payrolls rose 130,000 and unemployment was 4.3%. Average hourly earnings increased 3.7% over the year, and the workweek lengthened by 0.1 hour. Those figures supported current labour income, but most of the new jobs came from a few areas: health care added 82,000, social assistance 42,000 and construction 33,000, while federal government and finance lost jobs. Annual benchmark revisions also reduced the estimated 2025 payroll gain from 584,000 to 181,000. I therefore did not treat one stronger month as a return to the earlier trend. [BLS, 11 February]

January CPI rose 0.2% in the month and 2.4% over the year; core inflation was 2.5%. Gasoline fell 3.2% in the month and 7.5% over the year, but electricity and utility gas were respectively 6.3% and 9.8% higher than a year earlier. Overall inflation was lower, but a household paying more for electricity or gas could still feel worse off. [BLS, 13 February]

The Federal Reserve's January lending survey explained why those improvements did not automatically produce more financed purchases. Banks reported weaker demand for most residential mortgages, auto loans and other consumer loans. They expected loan performance to deteriorate during 2026 for small-business lending, residential real estate and most consumer categories, but not for large-company loans. Standards for business credit tightened, and approval appetite depended increasingly on the lender's view of the borrower's future exposure. [Federal Reserve lending survey, 2 February]

Answer

Better jobs and lower inflation helped households with their current income and bills. Buying a home or car required more than that: borrowers also needed savings, collateral and confidence about future employment, while banks had to expect repayment. Both sides were still cautious. The monthly improvement could support ordinary spending before it led to more mortgage or car borrowing.

Bond markets looked calmer than the bank survey. High-yield spreads widened slightly but remained below their November close, so listed companies could still borrow without a large increase in market rates. Small firms and households cannot issue bonds instead of taking a bank loan. Their borrowing could therefore become harder even while the main credit-market index remained calm.

United States

Better income, weak borrowing

January hiring improved, but the revision changed the context

The 130,000 payroll increase was a clear improvement from the 50,000 initially reported for December. The annual benchmark changed the context: total job growth during 2025 was revised down to 181,000, equivalent to about 15,000 a month. Long-term unemployment and involuntary part-time work were also higher than a year earlier. The January report therefore said hiring had improved in that month, not that the earlier slowdown had disappeared.

At the same time, banks expected loan performance to weaken in most consumer categories and reported less demand for mortgages, autos and other household loans. These answers can coexist with job growth. A family can continue ordinary spending from wages while delaying the down payment and multi-year obligation attached to a house or vehicle. A lender can approve daily card spending while becoming less comfortable with a large secured loan.

The survey also found that banks were more willing to approve firms they expected to benefit from artificial intelligence and less willing to approve those they thought could be harmed. This did not mean technology spending had suddenly become a new source of growth. It showed that a bank's view of a company's business model was beginning to affect whether that company could borrow.

My view

The jobs report reduced the immediate risk of falling labour income, but it did not bring back demand for mortgages, cars or small-business loans. I wanted to see those three areas improve together. If employment stayed positive while borrowing remained weak, the economy could still grow, but households and firms would continue to delay larger purchases and projects.

United Kingdom

Business investment fell

GDP grew slightly while business investment fell

GDP grew 0.1% in the fourth quarter and 1.3% across 2025. December output also rose 0.1%, with service growth offsetting falls in production and construction. The economy avoided contraction, but firms did not act as if the improvement was secure. The provisional estimate showed business investment falling 2.7% from the previous quarter, although it remained 2.0% above a year earlier. [ONS GDP, 12 February] [ONS business investment, 12 February]

The estimate was early and likely to be revised; its survey response rate was 62.5%, and large projects can move quarterly totals. That uncertainty argues for following subsequent estimates, not ignoring the result. A firm choosing not to buy equipment or software reduces current orders and future capacity even while existing services keep GDP just above zero.

The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% by 5–4, with four members preferring a cut. January CPI subsequently fell to 3.0%. Neither event guaranteed that capex would return: the expected revenue from a project can matter more than a small change in its financing rate. [Bank of England, 5 February]

My view

The UK avoided contraction, but firms spent less on future capacity. The estimate was preliminary, so I would not treat the 2.7% fall as final; I also would not ignore it because investment was still higher than a year earlier. An upward revision and stronger orders would support January's improvement. Another quarterly fall would suggest that firms remained doubtful.

China

Customer payments slowed

Producer prices improved before orders or payment times did

January's manufacturing PMI fell to 49.3 and the composite PMI to 49.8. New orders and employment were below 50. Producer prices declined 1.4% from a year earlier but increased 0.4% from December. The monthly price rise was useful: it suggested that the long decline in factory-gate prices might be losing force. It arrived before evidence that orders had crossed back above the expansion threshold. [NBS PMI, 2 February] [NBS PPI, 12 February]

Full-year industrial profits rose 0.6% in 2025. Manufacturing profits increased 5.0%, private-enterprise profits were flat, and the average collection period for receivables lengthened by 3.6 days. A company can report a sale and accounting profit before receiving cash. Longer collection ties up working capital and increases the need for short-term finance, especially when input costs begin to rise. [NBS industrial profits, 28 January]

My view

The monthly rise in producer prices was encouraging, but firms still had weak orders and waited longer for customers to pay. Private-company profits were also flat. I wanted selling prices, new orders and payment times to improve together. That would show that firms were receiving more cash, not simply putting a higher price on invoices that remained unpaid.

Underfollowed indicator

Banks lend according to the losses they expect later

Loan demand shows whether people and firms want to borrow, while lending standards show the rules banks apply. Those rules also depend on the losses banks expect later. February's survey found that banks expected more problems in small-company, mortgage and most consumer loans, but not in large-company loans. This helps explain why one good jobs or inflation report did not immediately make banks more willing to approve every borrower.

What continued

What changed

Weak loan demand helped explain why better current income did not lead to more home and car purchases.

What else weakened

What would change my view

Stronger loan demand, easier terms for small borrowers and faster customer payments would show an improvement.
