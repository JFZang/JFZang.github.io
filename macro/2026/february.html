Monthly Macro Highlights

The US is still creating jobs, Britain has avoided contraction, and China’s softer surveys sit beside tentative improvement in factory prices.

February 2026Reporting window: 24 January–20 February 2026By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

The data have become less threatening without becoming genuinely strong. Inflation is easing, but businesses and households still appear selective about hiring, investment and spending.

Global

A narrow path is becoming slightly wider

Lower inflation gives central banks more flexibility, yet the case for rapid easing is not settled. US core inflation is 2.5%, UK headline inflation has fallen to 3.0%, and China’s surveys point to a softer start even as monthly producer prices improve. [BLS, 13 February] [ONS, 18 February] [NBS, January PMI] [NBS, January PPI] The available figures describe different stages of adjustment rather than one synchronized cycle.

The market tension is between improving discount rates and mediocre underlying growth. Lower bond yields can support valuations, but they do not create end-demand. The closer policy rates move towards neutral, the more investors will need earnings rather than multiple expansion to carry returns.

My view

February makes a soft landing more plausible because US inflation is less awkward and the Bank of England is closer to easing. It still looks like a shallow landing rather than a vigorous rebound. Lower inflation can raise real wages, but households will spend that gain only if they remain confident about work; lower rates also reach mortgages and company borrowing gradually. I would become more cyclical if job breadth, credit demand and new orders improved together. Until then, businesses that can grow without a broad consumer upswing remain easier to underwrite.

Policy and market implications

A close vote captures the uncertainty

The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% by 5–4, with four members preferring a cut to 3.5%. It expects inflation to fall towards 2% from April, but remains concerned about whether wage and services inflation settle there sustainably. [Bank of England, 5 February]

For markets, the split is more informative than the unchanged rate. It points to further easing if demand weakens, but also warns against assuming a smooth sequence of cuts. Sterling, gilts and rate-sensitive UK equities may react to the same data in different ways.

United States

Cooling gently

Jobs recover while inflation becomes less awkward

January payrolls rose by 130,000 and unemployment was 4.3%. Health care, social assistance and construction added jobs, while federal government and financial activities contracted. [BLS, 11 February]

January CPI rose 0.2% on the month and 2.4% over the year. Core inflation was 2.5% year on year. [BLS, 13 February]

My view

This is close to the mix the Fed wants: employment is expanding without obvious overheating and inflation is moving nearer to target. The concern is that job gains are concentrated in only a few industries, which makes overall employment more vulnerable if one of them slows. I would therefore watch hours, vacancies and hiring across sectors rather than celebrate one headline.

United Kingdom

Barely growing

Avoiding recession is a low bar

GDP grew 0.1% in the fourth quarter and by 1.3% across 2025. December output rose 0.1%, with services growth offset by falls in production and construction. [ONS, 12 February]

January CPI inflation fell to 3.0%, while core CPI eased to 3.1%. [ONS, 18 February]

My view

Lower inflation is welcome, but the growth figures still describe an economy short of momentum. Lower rates should help gradually by reducing mortgage and business borrowing costs, although existing loans reprice with a delay. They cannot by themselves solve weak productivity or persuade firms to invest when demand remains uncertain.

China

Soft start, firmer prices

Activity weakens as factory deflation eases

January’s manufacturing PMI fell to 49.3, while the composite PMI slipped below the no-change line to 49.8. New orders and employment were both in contraction territory, pointing to a soft start to the year. [NBS, January PMI]

Consumer inflation remained subdued: CPI rose 0.2% year on year and core CPI increased 0.8%. Producer-price deflation eased, with PPI down 1.4% from a year earlier but up 0.4% on the month. [NBS, January CPI] [NBS, January PPI]

My view

The PMI weakness is most useful as a warning about breadth: smaller manufacturers appear to have entered the year with less demand than the large technology firms highlighted in earlier issues. The rise in monthly producer prices may be an early sign that price pressure is improving, but Lunar New Year timing can distort both production and orders. I would therefore wait for the combined January–February data before calling a turn. A broader rise in orders and selling prices would matter more than another strong output figure from a few industries.

What to watch

Evidence, not relief

Job breadth

US gains are concentrated in health care, social assistance and construction. Wider private-sector hiring would make the soft landing more durable.

UK real spending

Lower inflation only helps growth if households spend part of the income gain. Retail volumes and consumer services will show whether they do.

China opening data

The combined January–February production and retail release will test whether the PMI weakness reflects seasonal timing and whether firmer factory prices are being matched by stronger demand.

Core services prices

Headline inflation can fall with energy, but services reveal domestic persistence. Continued easing would give central banks genuine room rather than temporary relief.
