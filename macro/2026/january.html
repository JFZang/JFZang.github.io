Monthly Macro Highlights

A stable headline
hides a weaker hand-off

The year begins with slower US hiring, little movement in Britain and a Chinese growth target met through an increasingly uneven mix.

January 2026Data through 23 January 2026By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

None of the three economies looks close to recession, yet none enters 2026 with broad-based momentum. The most important signals sit beneath the headline numbers: hiring concentration in the US, services-led resilience in Britain and a widening production-demand gap in China.

Global

The new year starts without a common engine

Growth has survived restrictive policy, but the hand-off is modest. US payrolls rose only 50,000 in December, Britain’s monthly rebound sits beside high services inflation, and China met its 5% annual target with fourth-quarter growth slowing to 4.5%. The investment case depends less on a synchronized rebound and more on identifying where demand is genuinely durable.

The policy backdrop has improved but is still restrictive. The Federal Reserve’s target range and Bank Rate both end December at an upper bound of 3.75%. That is relief compared with the peak, not cheap capital. Companies facing near-term refinancing still have to earn through a higher cost base, while cash-rich firms retain a meaningful advantage.

My view

I expect continued expansion, but not the kind that lifts every cyclical business. Balance-sheet strength and pricing discipline matter more than broad optimism. My upside case requires lower inflation to lift real spending without a further loss of jobs; my downside case is that weak hiring makes households defensive before rate relief reaches them. I would rather miss the first part of a broad rally than assume that lower policy rates automatically repair uneven demand.

Markets and policy

The cost of capital is falling slowly, not disappearing

December’s rate cuts put the Federal Reserve target range at 3.5–3.75% and Bank Rate at 3.75%. The shared level can be misleading: the US faces a cooling labour market with 2.7% inflation, while the UK still has 3.4% inflation and higher services pressure. [Federal Reserve, 10 December] [Bank of England, 18 December]

That divergence is why currency and bond moves deserve a macro reading, not just a valuation reading. Different inflation paths imply different real rates even when nominal policy rates look similar.

United States

Slower hiring

The labour market is bending, not breaking

Payrolls rose by 50,000 in December and unemployment was 4.4%. Job gains were concentrated in restaurants, health care and social assistance, while retail employment fell. [BLS, 9 January]

December CPI rose 0.3% on the month and 2.7% over the year; core CPI increased 0.2% in the month. [BLS, 13 January]

My view

The combination is soft enough to keep rate cuts in view, but not weak enough to force them. Most of the job gains came from a small group of service industries, so the headline may overstate the strength of labour demand across the economy. I would pay more attention to how many industries are hiring than to whether one payroll number beats expectations.

United Kingdom

Low momentum

Services prevent a cleaner slowdown

GDP grew 0.3% in November and 0.1% over the three months to November. Monthly services output also rose 0.3%, while construction fell 1.3%. [ONS, 15 January]

December CPI inflation was 3.4%, with services inflation remaining elevated. [ONS, 21 January]

My view

The UK needs lower inflation more than it needs a dramatic change in interest-rate policy. Services prices matter because they are closely linked to domestic wages and tend to fall more slowly than goods prices. Until they cool more convincingly, households face continued pressure and the Bank of England has reason to cut rates cautiously.

China

Target met, mix uneven

Five per cent growth came with a softer finish

GDP grew 5.0% in 2025, while fourth-quarter growth slowed to 4.5%. Manufacturing expanded 6.1% over the year, compared with a 1.1% contraction in construction. [NBS, 21 January]

My view

The target has been achieved, but the composition is more revealing than the total. Advanced manufacturing remains a strength, while weak construction and cautious domestic demand mean that growth still relies heavily on production and external markets. That mix can sustain GDP, but it also increases exposure to foreign demand and does less to rebuild household confidence.

What I am watching

The quality of the hand-off

US revisions

Payroll momentum is already weak, so benchmark and monthly revisions matter unusually much. Further downgrades would change a slowdown into a clearer labour warning.

UK services prices

Services inflation links wages to the domestic price cycle. It is the main constraint on faster easing even while headline growth remains subdued.

China consumption

Manufacturing grew 6.1% in 2025 while construction contracted. Retail and household-income data will show whether policy is reaching the other side of the economy.

Credit transmission

Lower policy rates matter only when borrowing costs and volumes respond. Mortgage approvals, corporate lending and refinancing spreads are the practical test.
