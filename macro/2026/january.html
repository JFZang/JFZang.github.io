Monthly Macro Highlights

US retail sales improved, UK retail volumes fell over the quarter, and Chinese household spending grew more slowly than income as the December slowdown stopped short of a synchronised contraction.

January 2026Reporting window: 20 December 2025–23 January 2026By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

The December warning did not spread evenly. US unemployment fell and retail sales improved, UK output rebounded but fourth-quarter retail volumes declined, and China met its growth target while household spending lagged income.

Global

The December slowdown does not spread uniformly

US payrolls rose only 50,000 in December, but unemployment fell to 4.4%. UK monthly output rebounded 0.3%, and China met its 5% annual growth target even as fourth-quarter growth slowed to 4.5%. [BLS, 9 January] [ONS, 15 January] [NBS, 21 January]

Household data add useful detail. US November retail sales rose 0.6% from October, UK retail volumes fell 0.3% across the fourth quarter, and Chinese real consumption expenditure grew 4.4% in 2025 compared with 5.0% real disposable-income growth. [US Census, 14 January] [ONS retail sales, 23 January] [NBS household survey, 20 January]

My view

January reverses parts of December rather than starting a common upswing. US retail sales and UK monthly output rose, but UK quarterly retail volumes fell and Chinese consumption grew more slowly than income. The improvement is real at the monthly level; its weakness is that it has not carried across time periods or countries.

Policy and market implications

The same policy rate does not mean the same constraint

US inflation ended December at 2.7%, compared with 3.4% in the UK, while the upper bound of the federal funds target and Bank Rate were both 3.75%. [BLS, 13 January] [ONS, 21 January]

The spending response was different too. US retail sales rose 0.6% in November, whereas UK volumes fell 0.3% over the fourth quarter despite a 0.4% December rebound. [US Census, 14 January] [ONS, 23 January] Policy affects economies through mortgages, company refinancing and credit availability, not through the headline rate alone.

My view

I would not infer the next central-bank move by subtracting current inflation from the policy rate. The more useful comparison is whether borrowing costs are falling where households and firms actually refinance, and whether they respond by spending or investing. January’s data show different transmissions despite the same nominal policy rate.

United States

Spending offsets hiring

Slow hiring coexists with firmer retail sales

December payrolls rose by 50,000 and unemployment fell to 4.4%. Job growth remained concentrated in health care and social assistance, while professional services and federal employment declined. [BLS, 9 January]

November retail and food-service sales rose 0.6% from October and 3.3% from a year earlier. Non-store retailers were up 7.2% year on year and food services 4.9%. [US Census, 14 January]

My view

Retail spending provides a counterweight to weak hiring, so December’s labour warning has not yet become a broader household contraction. The risk is timing: spending can hold up briefly after recruitment slows. Hours, income and subsequent retail revisions will show whether consumers are drawing on current earnings or simply delaying the adjustment.

United Kingdom

Monthly rebound, weak quarter

One monthly rebound does not fix a weak quarter

GDP grew 0.3% in November but only 0.1% over the latest three months. Monthly services output rose 0.3%, while construction fell 1.3%. December CPI inflation was 3.4% and services inflation 4.5%. [ONS GDP, 15 January] [ONS CPI, 21 January]

Retail volumes rose 0.4% in December but fell 0.3% across the fourth quarter. Annual volumes were 1.3% higher than in 2024 yet remained below 2019. [ONS retail sales, 23 January]

My view

The November GDP rebound and December retail gain are welcome, but the quarterly comparisons remain weak. This looks more like households and services avoiding a further fall than starting a broad upswing. I would want quarterly retail volumes and construction to turn positive together before treating the monthly rebound as durable.

China

Income leads spending

Five per cent growth came with slower household spending

GDP grew 5.0% in 2025, while fourth-quarter growth slowed to 4.5%. Manufacturing expanded 6.1% over the year and construction contracted 1.1%. [NBS, 21 January]

Real disposable income rose 5.0% in 2025, while real household consumption expenditure rose 4.4%. Property income increased only 1.6%; housing expenditure grew 2.1%, compared with 9.4% for education, culture and recreation. [NBS household survey, 20 January]

My view

The annual target was met, but the household data show why the composition still matters. Consumption grew more slowly than income and property income was weak, while spending shifted towards selected services. That is more specific than saying demand lags production: households were earning more, but the housing-related wealth channel and the willingness to spend remained subdued.

What to watch

Tests for the start of 2026

US income and spending

Retail sales improved despite weak hiring. Hours and wage income will show whether that spending can continue without stronger job creation.

UK quarterly demand

Monthly GDP and retail both rebounded, but their quarterly growth remained weak. Business investment will show whether firms share the short-term improvement.

China saving behaviour

Income grew faster than consumption and property income was subdued. The household saving rate and housing activity will show whether caution persists.

Bank lending

December’s rate cuts matter only if loan prices and approvals change. Mortgage, consumer and business credit should be read separately.
