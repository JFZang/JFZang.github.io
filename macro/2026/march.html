Monthly Macro Highlights

The first economic effect of the Middle East shock appeared in shipping rather than CPI. Tanker rates, new freight charges and trapped vessels showed how the disruption was beginning to reach businesses.

March 2026Reporting window: 21 February–27 March 2026By Jiefu Zhang

Central question

How did the energy and shipping shock enter company costs, inventories and contracts?

The March data showed a clear sequence. Unsafe passage reduced the number of available ships. Carriers then added charges for insurance, longer routes and more expensive fuel. Inventories and emergency oil reserves gave some buyers more time, while regulated household energy prices were expected to change later.

What changed this month

The disruption spread from oil into shipping

3,200 vessels confined

As of 13 March, the IMO reported about 3,200 vessels confined west of the Strait of Hormuz and roughly 20,000 seafarers affected.

Freight contracts repriced

Maersk suspended selected Upper Gulf bookings and applied emergency freight and bunker surcharges to cargo already booked as well as new shipments.

400 million barrels released

IEA members agreed their largest collective stock release, but warned that regular passage through the strait was still the necessary route back to stable flows.

Tanker capacity tightened globally

Middle East-to-Asia crude tanker rates reached their highest level in a data series beginning in November 2005.

Evidence and mechanism

Less shipping capacity led to higher charges and new contract terms

The immediate problem was not a forecast of future scarcity. It was the inability to move existing cargo safely. The IMO said attacks and navigational risk had left thousands of ships confined in the Gulf. UN Trade and Development estimated that the strait normally carries about one quarter of global seaborne oil trade as well as important LNG and fertiliser flows. [IMO, 16 March] [UNCTAD, 10 March]

The shipping problem quickly appeared in company invoices. Maersk introduced an emergency freight increase of USD 1,800 for a 20-foot dry container and USD 3,000 for a 40-foot dry container on affected routes. From 25 March it also applied a worldwide emergency bunker surcharge of USD 200 and USD 400 respectively on long-haul headhaul shipments. The carrier said the charges covered longer routes, fuel bought at war premiums and costs outside its normal fuel-fee formula. It also used a contract clause that allowed rates to change for cargo already in its possession. [Maersk, 16 March]

Oil reserves could cushion the volume loss but could not replace ships, insurance or route capacity. On 11 March, IEA members agreed to release 400 million barrels after crude and product exports through the strait fell below 10% of their pre-conflict level. In the United States, commercial crude stocks rose from 443.1 million barrels on 6 March to 456.2 million on 20 March. Those barrels reduced immediate US scarcity; they did not remove the global freight bottleneck. [IEA, 11 March] [EIA, weekly crude stocks]

Answer

Shipping gave the earliest answer. When fewer vessels could use the route safely, tanker rates rose and carriers added charges for fuel, insurance and longer journeys. Companies with larger inventories could wait longer than those needing immediate deliveries. Household utility bills would change later because the UK price cap was set on a schedule, and CPI would record the result after that.

Market dashboard

Oil, freight, equities and credit showed different parts of the shock

Brent: USD 121.47

The European spot benchmark reached USD 121.47 per barrel on 27 March, reflecting the scarcity of internationally deliverable crude.

VLCC rates: series high

Middle East-to-Asia very large crude carrier rates were the highest since the EIA series began in November 2005.

S&P 500: −6.9%

The index fell from 6,837.75 on 23 February to 6,368.85 on 27 March as investors expected weaker profits and became less willing to take risk.

US high-yield spread: +47bp

The option-adjusted spread rose from 2.95% on 23 February, the first available observation in the window, to 3.42% on 27 March.

United States

Inventories delayed shortages

US oil inventories provided time, but did not solve the shipping problem

US commercial crude inventories increased by 13.1 million barrels between 6 and 20 March. The IEA release added more emergency supply. When the Federal Reserve held rates on 18 March, it said the effect of the Middle East developments on the US economy was still uncertain. [EIA, weekly crude stocks] [Federal Reserve, 18 March]

My view

The rise in US crude inventories helps explain why the shipping disruption did not immediately create the same shortage inside the United States. Those stocks only bought time. They would become less helpful if the route stayed restricted or if the right fuel products could not reach the regions that needed them.

United Kingdom

Household bills changed later

Wholesale gas rose before household utility bills could change

The Bank of England said European wholesale gas was about 60% above its pre-conflict level before its March meeting. UK gas futures feeding into the July–September Ofgem price cap had risen by an average of 35–40%. The April–June cap was already fixed, so the first household utility effect was delayed even as fuel prices rose immediately. [Bank of England, 19 March]

My view

The UK price-cap schedule meant that wholesale gas prices could rise well before household bills. March was therefore too early to expect the full effect in consumer spending. I was watching the futures prices used for the next cap rather than assuming that current retail data already included the shock.

China

More cash was tied up

Chinese firms already had more money tied up in goods and unpaid bills

At the end of February, finished-goods inventories at large industrial enterprises were 6.6% higher than a year earlier and accounts receivable were 7.1% higher. Inventory turnover lengthened by 0.4 day and the average collection period by 1.3 days. February producer purchasing prices rose 0.7% from January even before the March shipping disruption had entered the data. [NBS industrial accounts, 27 March] [NBS producer prices, 10 March]

My view

A manufacturer holding more unsold goods and waiting longer to be paid has less cash available for higher freight and material costs. March's data were too early to measure the conflict's effect on Chinese profits. They did show which firms would find the extra cost harder to absorb.

Underfollowed indicator

New freight charges appeared before official inflation data

A commodity price shows the cost where the product is traded. A carrier surcharge shows whether that cost has entered a customer's invoice, which routes it covers and whether an existing customer can avoid it. The March notices applied to cargo already booked or controlled by the carrier, and the bunker surcharge extended beyond Gulf routes. The contracts therefore showed higher business costs before CPI or quarterly profit data could record them.

Since last month

The new problem was the cash tied up in goods and shipping

What continued

Borrowers were already cautious in February, and March gave them a new reason to hold more cash.

What changed

Limited shipping, higher insurance and new contract charges could raise costs even without weaker domestic demand.

Still unclear

Official price and profit data had not had time to record the full shock by 27 March.

What would change my view

Normal shipping, removed surcharges and lower tanker rates would suggest that the increase in costs was short-lived.

Timeline

How the March shock entered the data

  • 2–3 March: Strait traffic was effectively halted and Maersk suspended selected cargo acceptance and bookings.
  • 10 March: UNCTAD documented the strait's role in oil, LNG and fertiliser trade.
  • 11 March: IEA members agreed to release 400 million barrels from emergency reserves.
  • 16 March: Maersk set out emergency freight increases and a worldwide bunker surcharge.
  • 18–19 March: The Federal Reserve and Bank of England held rates; the IMO met in an extraordinary session on shipping safety.
  • 26 March: The EIA reported multi-decade highs in Middle East crude tanker rates.
