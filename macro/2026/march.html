Monthly Macro Highlights

An energy shock hit as US retail sales softened, UK households pulled spending forward, and Chinese investment relied more on state and equipment spending than private capital.

March 2026Reporting window: 21 February–27 March 2026By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

March shifts the focus from domestic data to the transmission of an external energy shock. Demand was already soft before the conflict intensified, so the duration of higher freight, utility and production costs matters more than the first move in oil.

Global

An energy shock raises prices and cuts purchasing power

Before the Middle East conflict intensified, US payrolls had fallen, UK output was flat and Chinese private investment was contracting. The energy shock then raised transport, utility and production costs while removing spending power from importing economies. [BLS, 6 March] [ONS, 13 March] [NBS investment, 16 March]

The Bank of England reported that expected policy paths moved higher and financial conditions tightened as investors reassessed the shock. The transmission goes beyond crude oil: freight, fertiliser, utilities and energy-intensive manufacturing face different lags and hedges. [Bank of England, 19 March]

My view

Duration changes who bears the cost. A short shock reduces household purchasing power and company margins; a longer one enters wage bargaining, freight contracts and corporate pricing. Because demand was already soft, importers have less scope to absorb the first effect through higher sales volumes. Wage settlements and margin guidance will reveal more about persistence than the oil price alone.

Policy and market implications

Oil, rates and equities reprice the shock

The Federal Reserve kept its target range at 3.5–3.75%, noting solid activity, low job gains and Middle East uncertainty. The Bank of England unanimously held Bank Rate at 3.75% after the energy shock interrupted the earlier fall in inflation. [Federal Reserve, 18 March] [Bank of England, 19 March]

The S&P 500 fell from 6,837.75 on 23 February to 6,368.85 on 27 March, a decline of about 6.9% during the reporting window. [FRED, S&P 500] Oil measures the direct external cost; rate expectations show the inflation response; equities capture the effect on expected profits and risk appetite.

My view

The equity decline confirms that the shock reached earnings expectations, but it does not tell us whether inflation or weaker demand will dominate. I would use the three prices together: persistently high oil, higher expected rates and further equity weakness would be a more stagflationary signal than any one move alone.

United States

Jobs and sales soften

A weak payroll print meets softer retail demand

Nonfarm payrolls fell by 92,000 in February and unemployment edged to 4.4%. Strike activity reduced health-care employment, while information and federal government jobs also declined. February CPI rose 0.3% on the month and 2.4% over the year. [BLS employment, 6 March] [BLS CPI, 11 March]

January retail and food-service sales fell 0.2% from December, although they remained 3.2% above a year earlier and non-store sales were up 10.9%. [US Census, 6 March]

My view

Strikes make the payroll headline less alarming, but softer retail sales mean I would not dismiss the month as a labour-only distortion. The annual spending comparison and online growth remain positive, so recession is too strong a conclusion. A broader fall across private hiring, hours and retail categories would be the evidence that changes it.

United Kingdom

Spending timing distorts

Households pull spending forward, then retrench

GDP was unchanged in January and grew 0.2% over the latest three months. February CPI inflation held at 3.0%, while services inflation eased to 4.3%. [ONS GDP, 13 March] [ONS CPI, 25 March]

Retail volumes fell 0.4% in February after a 2.0% January rise, as some spending had been brought forward. Volumes were still 0.7% higher over three months, and online values were 11.4% above a year earlier. [ONS retail sales, 27 March]

My view

The monthly retail fall is not a clean demand collapse because January pulled purchases forward and the three-month measure stayed positive. It does show that consumers were sensitive to timing before the energy shock arrived. Higher fuel and utility bills now have a narrow growth cushion to work against.

China

State capital leads

State investment, not private investment, drives the gain

Industrial value added grew 6.3% year on year across January and February, while retail sales rose 2.8%. Online goods sales increased 10.3%. [NBS industrial production] [NBS retail sales]

Fixed-asset investment rose 1.8%, but the ownership split was sharp: state-holding investment increased 7.7% and private investment fell 2.6%. Equipment purchases rose 11.5% and infrastructure 11.4%. [NBS fixed-asset investment, 16 March]

My view

The combined data answer February’s seasonal question: output held up, but the investment support came mainly from state-controlled and equipment spending. That can sustain production without showing that private firms have regained confidence. I would look for private investment and new orders to turn positive before calling the recovery self-sustaining.

What to watch

How the energy shock reaches the economy

Duration and hedging

Spot prices move first; freight contracts, utility tariffs and corporate hedges change later. Those lags determine the hit to margins and household bills.

Real retail volumes

Nominal sales can rise with fuel prices. Volume measures will show whether households are buying more or paying more.

China private investment

State and equipment spending led the opening gain. A turn in private investment would show that support is spreading beyond directed capital.

Cross-asset confirmation

Oil, expected policy rates, equities and credit should be read together. Persistent moves across all four would signal a deeper repricing than an isolated commodity jump.
