Monthly Macro Highlights

Energy rewrites
the landing debate

A new supply shock lands on an already uneven backdrop of softer US hiring, flat UK output and production-led Chinese growth.

March 2026Reporting window: 21 February–27 March 2026By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

The month began with improving disinflation and ends with a fresh energy shock. Softer US hiring, flat UK output and China’s production-heavy start now have to be assessed alongside higher fuel costs and tighter financial conditions.

Global

The soft-landing story meets a supply shock

Before the Middle East conflict intensified, the data pointed to easing inflation but uneven demand: US payrolls fell, Britain was barely growing, and Chinese production again outpaced consumption. The sharp rise in global energy and other commodity prices changes the near-term balance. It raises headline inflation while taking purchasing power away from households—a difficult combination for central banks.

The initial market response is economically important in its own right. The Bank of England reports that expected policy paths moved higher and financial conditions tightened as investors reassessed energy risk. This is not only an oil story: transport, utilities, fertiliser and energy-intensive manufacturing all transmit the shock differently across countries and company margins. [Bank of England, 19 March]

My view

I still see expansion as the base case, but the range around it has widened. The first-round price rise is less important than duration and behaviour: if energy settles quickly, weaker demand may dominate and disinflation can resume; if it remains high, wage demands and corporate repricing could make the shock persistent. I would not treat every energy producer as a winner or every consumer business as a loser. Balance sheets, hedging and the ability to protect volumes will decide who absorbs the cost.

Policy and market implications

Central banks pause for a different reason

The Federal Reserve kept its target range at 3.5–3.75%, noting solid activity, low job gains and uncertainty from the Middle East. The Bank of England unanimously held Bank Rate at 3.75% after the energy shock altered an otherwise improving inflation picture. [Federal Reserve, 18 March] [Bank of England, 19 March]

This is why oil and market-implied rates deserve attention together. Oil measures the size of the external shock; the rate curve shows whether investors think it will create persistent inflation or mainly weaken demand.

United States

Hiring cools

One weak payroll print deserves attention, not panic

Nonfarm payrolls fell by 92,000 in February and unemployment edged to 4.4%. Strike activity reduced health-care employment, while information and federal government jobs also declined. [BLS, 6 March]

February CPI rose 0.3% on the month and 2.4% over the year; core prices increased 0.2% in the month and 2.5% over twelve months. [BLS, 11 March]

My view

Strike activity and falling federal employment explain part of the payroll decline, which makes the headline less alarming. The margin for error has still narrowed because job creation outside those effects is not especially strong. If weakness spreads across private industries, hours and claims, the case for easier policy strengthens quickly.

United Kingdom

Flat output

Better inflation, little momentum

GDP was unchanged in January, although output grew 0.2% over the latest three months. Services were flat, production slipped 0.1%, and construction rose 0.2%. [ONS, 13 March]

February CPI inflation held at 3.0%; services inflation eased to 4.3%, its lowest rate since March 2022. [ONS, 25 March]

My view

The inflation details are moving in the right direction, but the economy has little cushion. Services inflation remains above the headline rate, so the Bank of England cannot assume domestic price pressure has disappeared. At the same time, stagnant activity means keeping rates high for too long could weaken employment and spending, which is why I expect a cautious balance.

China

Industry ahead

The familiar imbalance returns

Industrial value added grew 6.3% year on year across January and February, including 13.1% growth in high-tech manufacturing. Retail sales rose a more modest 2.8%, although online goods sales increased 10.3%. [NBS, industrial production] [NBS, retail sales]

My view

The combined January–February data answer February’s timing question: activity has momentum, but production is still doing more work than household demand. Unsold capacity eventually has to be exported, discounted or left idle, bringing trade friction, thinner margins or weaker investment. I remain more positive on advanced manufacturers that can win share through better products than on firms relying on a general domestic recovery. Stronger private demand, rather than another production target, would change that balance.

What to watch

Signals for the second quarter

Energy duration

A short spike mainly changes headline inflation; a prolonged disruption reaches wages, margins and expectations. Duration matters more than the first price move.

US hiring

Strike effects explain part of February’s decline. Weakness across claims, hours and private-sector industries would show that the slowdown is broader.

UK pass-through

The UK imports energy and already has elevated services inflation. Fuel, utility and wage-setting data will show whether the shock becomes domestic.

China demand mix

Production is growing more than twice as fast as retail sales. A wider gap would increase dependence on exports precisely as global demand faces another cost shock.
