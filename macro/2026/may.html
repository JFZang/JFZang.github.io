Monthly Macro Highlights

Three economies,
three different engines

The major economies are still expanding, yet their engines look increasingly different: US employment, UK services and Chinese manufacturing are carrying more than their share.

May 2026Reporting window: 25 April–22 May 2026By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

May’s data do not point to a synchronised global cycle. The US still has enough demand to keep inflation uncomfortable, the UK has produced a better run of service-sector growth, and China’s industrial capacity continues to move faster than household spending.

Global

The common energy shock is producing different economies

April data no longer support a single global description. US payroll growth slowed to 115,000. [BLS employment, 8 May] Inflation rose to 3.8%. [BLS CPI, 12 May] UK inflation fell to 2.8% as first-quarter GDP grew 0.6%. [ONS, 20 May] In China, industrial output increased 4.1% and high-technology manufacturing 12.8%, but retail sales were almost flat at 0.2%. [NBS industrial production] [NBS retail sales]

The difference comes from where demand is generated. US households still have employment income but face renewed price pressure; UK services are benefiting from improving real incomes; Chinese factories can expand even when consumers remain cautious.

My view

The global expansion is intact, but broad conclusions about the whole market are becoming less useful. US inflation, UK services and Chinese manufacturing are being driven by different forces, so they will not respond to the same policy signal in the same way. I therefore want to know where a company’s demand comes from, how much depends on government support, and whether it can absorb higher costs without losing customers.

Policy and market implications

The shock is moving from prices to behaviour

By May, the relevant question is no longer whether energy prices jumped, but how companies and households respond. The Federal Reserve kept its target range unchanged in late April, while the Bank of England had already held Bank Rate at 3.75% in April with one member preferring an increase. [Federal Reserve, 29 April] [Bank of England, 30 April]

That caution explains why falling market volatility would not by itself settle the macro question. Confirmation would require stable inflation expectations, credit spreads and household spending together; calm markets can coexist with a slow deterioration in the real economy.

United States

Demand still firm

Employment slows without giving way

April payrolls rose by 115,000 and unemployment stayed at 4.3%. Health care, transport and retail added jobs, while federal employment continued to fall. Average hourly earnings were 3.6% higher than a year earlier. [BLS, 8 May]

April CPI rose 0.6% on the month and 3.8% over the year. Energy and shelter were the main contributors, while core inflation remained less severe than the headline move. [BLS, 12 May]

My view

This looks more like moderation than labour-market failure. The difficulty is that slower hiring has not yet produced comfortably low inflation. Rate-sensitive businesses should not build plans around rapid easing; wage growth and shelter costs still leave the Fed with reasons to wait.

United Kingdom

Better momentum

Services deliver a stronger first quarter

GDP grew 0.3% in March and 0.6% across the first quarter. Services rose 0.8% over the three months, with growth spread across most subsectors. [ONS, 14 May]

April CPI inflation fell to 2.8% from 3.3%, helped by lower household energy prices and softer services inflation. Motor fuel prices moved the other way, reminding investors that the improvement is not guaranteed to continue. [ONS, 20 May]

My view

The UK picture has improved more than its reputation. Still, a service-led rebound does not remove weak productivity or household sensitivity to rates. I see selective opportunity in domestic businesses with operating leverage, but only where balance sheets can tolerate a slower policy response.

China

Supply outruns demand

Technology manufacturing remains the bright spot

Industrial value added grew 4.1% year on year in April, including 12.8% growth in high-technology manufacturing. Electronics output rose 15.6%, while construction-related materials remained weak. [NBS industrial production]

Retail sales rose only 0.2% year on year, or 1.8% excluding autos. Autos, appliances, furniture and building materials all declined. CPI rose 1.2% over the year. [NBS retail sales] [NBS CPI]

My view

April’s category detail makes the split more concrete. Electronics are expanding, while autos, appliances, furniture and building materials are falling—the areas most exposed to household confidence and property. Export-capable technology manufacturers can therefore grow without a broad domestic recovery, but consumer and property-linked firms cannot borrow the technology sector’s momentum. I would want the weakness across those retail categories to narrow before treating industrial strength as representative of China as a whole.

What to watch

Signals for June

US hiring breadth

Health care and a few services are carrying payroll growth. Broader private hiring would show that firm consumption has a durable income base.

UK follow-through

March services were strong, but one month can reflect timing. New business, hours worked and April output will test whether the improvement persists.

China retail

The test extends beyond subsidised goods and technology categories. A wider recovery would suggest household confidence, rather than policy incentives alone, is improving.

Energy pass-through

Producer prices, freight and corporate guidance will reveal whether higher energy is being absorbed, passed to customers or met with lower production.
