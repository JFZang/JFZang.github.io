Monthly Macro Highlights

Equities recovered and credit spreads narrowed after March's sharp sell-off. Early company results were encouraging, but only a small share had reported and oil was still expensive.

April 2026Reporting window: 28 March–24 April 2026By Jiefu Zhang

Central question

Did the market rebound because the shock was fading, or because fundamentals improved?

Most of the market move came from investors becoming less worried about a long disruption. Volatility fell and credit spreads narrowed. The first company results were also strong, but only 10% of the S&P 500 had reported, so they could not yet prove that profits were improving across the index. Oil remained expensive and households had not seen the same recovery.

What changed this month

Markets recovered before the oil shortage had cleared

S&P 500: +13.0%

The index rose from 6,343.72 on 30 March to 7,165.08 on 24 April, more than reversing the previous month's fall.

High-yield spread: 3.46% → 2.86%

The US high-yield option-adjusted spread narrowed by 60 basis points, removing most of the March risk repricing.

Early blended Q1 EPS: +13.2%

Only 10% of S&P 500 companies had reported; the figure combined those results with estimates for the rest, while blended revenue growth was 9.9%.

Spot oil still scarce

Dated Brent traded at a premium of more than USD 25 to the front futures contract in early April.

Evidence and mechanism

Lower fear and early earnings both helped the rebound

The clearest change was that investors became less afraid of a long disruption. The S&P 500 gained 13.0% during the window, the high-yield spread narrowed from 3.46% to 2.86%, and the VIX fell from 30.61 on 30 March to 18.71 on 24 April. Equities, credit and volatility all moved in the same direction. [FRED, S&P 500] [FRED, high-yield spread] [FRED, VIX]

Early company results also helped, although the evidence was incomplete. By 17 April, only 10% of S&P 500 companies had reported. FactSet's blended earnings growth rate—which combined those results with estimates for the remaining companies—was 13.2%, unchanged from quarter-end; blended revenue growth was 9.9%. The sample did not show a sudden profit collapse, but it was too small to describe the whole index. The forward price-to-earnings ratio also rose from 19.7 at the end of March to 20.9, meaning investors paid more for each dollar of expected profit. [FactSet, 17 April]

The oil market was less reassuring. Dated Brent cost more than USD 25 above the front-month futures contract, showing that buyers were paying much more for oil available immediately even though later prices suggested better supply. Spot Brent was still USD 111.86 on 24 April. Investors could expect the disruption to ease later while companies and households continued to pay the current high price. [EIA, 24 April] [EIA, Brent spot price]

Answer

The rebound was not simply noise, but it also did not prove that the shock was over. Lower volatility and narrower credit spreads showed that investors were less worried about a prolonged disruption. The first company results did not show a sudden profit collapse, although the sample was small. Higher valuations still depended on oil and transport costs falling before they caused lasting damage to demand.

Market dashboard

Market prices and company results did not say exactly the same thing

Equities

A 13.0% rise showed a large change in the probability attached to the March shock.

Credit

A 60-basis-point spread tightening showed that default risk did not confirm March's worst market scenario.

Valuation

The forward P/E rose to 20.9, meaning part of the move came from a higher multiple, not higher earnings alone.

Oil curve

Extreme backwardation said immediate barrels were scarce while the futures market expected conditions to improve later.

United States

Early earnings were positive

Early results from large companies were encouraging but incomplete

Within the 10% of S&P 500 companies that had reported, 88% beat earnings estimates and 84% beat revenue estimates. Those rates described a strong opening sample, not the breadth of a completed reporting season. FactSet still projected or reported earnings growth in eight of eleven sectors and revenue growth in all eleven. Together, the figures argued against an immediate profit break without proving that the strength already extended across the full index. [FactSet, 17 April]

Household evidence was less clean. March retail and food-service sales rose 1.7%, but the figures were nominal and energy prices increased 10.9% in the same month. Part of the apparent spending strength was a higher bill rather than more goods. [US Census, 21 April] [BLS, 10 April]

My view

Early earnings helped explain part of the US rally, but only 10% of companies had reported. Household spending was also less impressive once the effect of higher prices was considered. I would feel more confident if later company reports kept revenue growth intact and if households bought more goods rather than simply paying higher prices.

United Kingdom

Data mostly came before the shock

Better output mostly described the period before the shock

February GDP rose 0.5%, with services, production and construction all contributing. That was useful evidence of momentum entering the shock, but it largely predated the disruption. March retail volumes then rose 0.7% because motorists stocked up on fuel; excluding fuel, the gain was only 0.2%. [ONS GDP, 16 April] [ONS retail sales, 24 April]

March CPI was 3.3% and services inflation rose to 4.5%. The data therefore did not support a simple claim that lower energy risk would quickly give the Bank of England room to offset the household squeeze. [ONS CPI, 22 April]

My view

The UK data showed that activity was improving before the disruption, not that it had recovered afterwards. Some of March's retail gain came from buying fuel early, while services inflation remained high. I wanted to see later non-fuel sales and company profits before treating the market rebound as an economic recovery.

China

Households and property lagged

China reached 5% growth, but households and property remained weak

First-quarter GDP grew 5.0% year on year. Information-technology services expanded 10.6% and industry 6.1%, while construction contracted 3.8%. Real household disposable income rose 4.0%, but real consumption expenditure increased 2.6%. [NBS Q1 GDP] [NBS household survey, 17 April]

Real-estate investment fell 11.2% and new starts fell 20.3%. This did not erase the GDP result; it explained why a solid aggregate number did not produce a broader improvement in confidence and spending. [NBS real estate, 17 April]

My view

Technology and industry supported China's 5% growth rate, but household spending and property were much weaker. The headline GDP number was therefore stronger than the parts of the economy most closely connected to household confidence. I would not treat the 5% result alone as evidence of a broad improvement in domestic demand.

Underfollowed indicator

The oil curve showed that current supply was still tight

A falling spot price alone can be read as improving supply or weaker demand. The gap between spot and futures is more informative. April's unusually large spot premium showed that buyers still competed for immediate delivery while expecting later supply to be easier. That is exactly the combination in which equities can rebound before operating costs normalise.

Since last month

April showed which parts of March's shock were starting to ease

What continued

The shock reached energy prices and spending: US receipts rose with prices, and UK motorists bought fuel early.

What changed

Markets became much less worried about a long disruption, while early earnings were better than feared.

Still unclear

Company profits and discretionary sales had not yet shown who absorbed the higher March costs.

What would change my view

Higher volatility, wider credit spreads and lower earnings forecasts together would suggest that April's relief came too early.
