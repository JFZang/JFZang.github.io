Monthly Macro Highlights

An energy shock interrupts
the soft-landing story

Activity has proved resilient across the US, UK and China, but a sudden rise in fuel costs has reopened questions that appeared to be settling only weeks ago.

April 2026Reporting window: 28 March–24 April 2026By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

April begins with reasonable growth and ends with a harder inflation problem. The first-round effect is visible in fuel prices. The more important question is whether households, companies and workers treat the shock as temporary or start to change spending, pricing and wage decisions.

Global

The baseline survives, but the risks have shifted

The IMF projects global growth of 3.1% in 2026 and 3.2% in 2027, assuming the Middle East conflict remains limited. It expects global headline inflation to rise modestly this year before resuming its decline. The downside scenario is a longer conflict, higher energy prices and tighter financial conditions. [IMF, 14 April]

My view

The base case remains expansion, but the range of outcomes is wider. I would not chase commodity exposure simply because prices have risen: a rapid supply response or de-escalation could reverse the move before higher prices become higher profits. The more durable opportunities are businesses that benefit from a lasting shortage and can convert it into cash flow without depending on the initial spike.

Policy and market implications

The shock has moved from markets into consumer prices

March CPI inflation reached 3.3% in both the US and UK, but the details were different. US core prices rose only 0.2% in the month even as gasoline surged, while UK services inflation increased to 4.5%. [BLS, 10 April] [ONS, 22 April]

That distinction changes the policy message. A fuel-led rise can fade if supply normalises; stronger services inflation is more closely tied to domestic wages and pricing decisions. Bond markets may react to the same 3.3% headline, but the Bank of England has more reason than the Fed to worry that the pressure will last. [BLS, 10 April] [ONS, 22 April]

United States

Resilient, less comfortable

Jobs recover as gasoline lifts inflation

March payrolls rose by 178,000 after a February decline, while unemployment was 4.3%. Health care, construction and transport added jobs; federal employment continued to fall. [BLS, 3 April]

March CPI jumped 0.9% on the month and 3.3% over the year. Energy rose 10.9%, led by a 21.2% increase in gasoline, while core CPI rose 0.2% on the month. [BLS, 10 April]

My view

The headline inflation shock is severe, but core inflation is the better guide to persistence. A healthy labour market makes the economy more resilient; it also gives the Fed less reason to look through the shock immediately. Rate cuts now depend on evidence that energy is not spreading into services and wages.

United Kingdom

Growth meets inflation

February was strong; March prices are the warning

GDP rose 0.5% in February, with services, production and construction all growing. Output increased 0.5% across the three months to February. [ONS, 16 April]

March CPI inflation rose to 3.3% from 3.0%, with motor fuels making the largest upward contribution. Services inflation also increased to 4.5%. [ONS, 22 April]

My view

The UK cannot take February’s broad growth for granted. Fuel costs hit disposable income quickly, while high services inflation limits the policy response. Companies exposed to essential spending should hold up better than those relying on a strong discretionary recovery.

China

Strong first quarter

Production leads a faster start to the year

First-quarter GDP grew 5.0% year on year and 1.3% from the previous quarter. Industry expanded 6.1%, while information technology services grew 10.6%; construction contracted 3.8%. [NBS Q1 GDP]

March industrial production rose 5.7% year on year, compared with 1.7% growth in retail sales. CPI was 1.0% higher than a year earlier. [NBS industrial production] [NBS retail sales] [NBS CPI]

My view

The first quarter is solid, but the mix still leans towards production. China’s manufacturers are well placed to capture external demand, while weak construction and softer retail categories point to continued caution at home. Imported energy adds another cost for producers and consumers.

What to watch

Where the shock travels next

Core inflation

A short energy spike mainly changes headline CPI. Persistent increases in transport and services would show that businesses are passing the shock through.

Real incomes

Fuel and utility bills reduce spending power quickly. Retail volumes and discretionary services will reveal how much demand households cut.

Corporate margins

Reported gross margins and guidance will separate firms with real pricing power from those protecting profit only by accepting lower volumes.

China exports

Manufacturing is stronger than domestic retail demand. Export orders will show whether foreign demand can absorb output as global costs rise.
