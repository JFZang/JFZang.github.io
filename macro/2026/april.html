Monthly Macro Highlights

The March energy shock reached US and UK prices, while retail data showed stronger US nominal spending and UK fuel stockpiling rather than a uniform collapse in demand.

April 2026Reporting window: 28 March–24 April 2026By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

April confirms the first-round energy effect but not a common second round. US core inflation stayed contained, UK services inflation rose, UK households stocked up on fuel, and Chinese real consumption grew more slowly than income.

Global

Headline spending rises for three different reasons

US retail and food-service sales rose 1.7% in March, but the figure was nominal and energy prices rose 10.9% in the same month. UK retail volumes increased 0.7%, led by fuel stockpiling; excluding fuel, the gain was only 0.2%. [US Census, 21 April] [BLS, 10 April] [ONS retail sales, 24 April]

China supplied a third pattern. Real disposable income rose 4.0% in the first quarter, while real household consumption expenditure rose only 2.6%; urban consumption grew 2.0% and rural consumption 3.7%. [NBS household survey, 17 April]

My view

The March spending totals overstate the improvement in underlying demand for different reasons: US values include higher prices, the UK volume gain includes precautionary fuel purchases, and Chinese consumption lagged income. I read April as a change in timing and composition, not a common increase in household appetite. Real ex-fuel and discretionary volumes are the cleaner evidence until those distortions pass.

Policy and market implications

Equities reverse the March sell-off before inflation is settled

March CPI inflation was 3.3% in both the US and UK, but US core prices rose only 0.2% while UK services inflation increased to 4.5%. A shared headline therefore gave the two central banks different domestic signals. [BLS, 10 April] [ONS, 22 April]

Equities moved much faster than the inflation evidence. The S&P 500 rose from 6,343.72 on 30 March to 7,165.08 on 24 April, reversing the March fall with a gain of about 13.0% during the reporting window. [FRED, S&P 500]

My view

The rebound says investors quickly reduced the probability of a long disruption; it does not prove that household or company costs have normalised. I would treat the market move as a change in the odds assigned to that disruption, then use services inflation, freight costs and margins to judge the slower economic transmission.

United States

Demand holds through shock

Jobs recover and nominal retail sales jump

March payrolls rose by 178,000 after February’s decline, and unemployment was 4.3%. CPI rose 0.9% in the month and 3.3% over the year; energy increased 10.9%, while core CPI rose 0.2%. [BLS employment, 3 April] [BLS CPI, 10 April]

Retail and food-service sales rose 1.7% in March and 4.0% from a year earlier. Non-store sales were 10.1% higher than a year earlier. [US Census, 21 April]

My view

The payroll rebound and retail gain reduce March’s immediate demand concern. But the sales data are nominal and arrived in the same month as a large gasoline increase, so part of the rise reflects prices rather than volume. I would read this as demand holding up through the first shock, not proof that households absorbed it without a real-income cost.

United Kingdom

Fuel distorts spending

Fuel stockpiling lifts retail sales

GDP rose 0.5% in February, with services, production and construction all growing. March CPI inflation then rose to 3.3%, and services inflation reached 4.5%. [ONS GDP, 16 April] [ONS CPI, 22 April]

Retail volumes rose 0.7% in March, but the gain was led by fuel as households stocked up. Excluding fuel, volumes increased only 0.2%. [ONS retail sales, 24 April]

My view

February’s broad GDP rise predates the fuel shock. March retail gives the first behavioural clue, and much of it was precautionary buying rather than stronger discretionary demand. The next test is whether non-fuel volumes and consumer services hold up after the stockpiling passes and higher fuel costs reach household budgets.

China

Households save more

Income rises faster than household consumption

First-quarter GDP grew 5.0% year on year. Industry expanded 6.1%, information-technology services 10.6%, and construction contracted 3.8%. [NBS Q1 GDP]

Real disposable income rose 4.0% in the first quarter, while real consumption expenditure rose 2.6%. Urban consumption grew 2.0% in real terms, compared with 3.7% in rural areas. Real-estate investment fell 11.2% and new starts fell 20.3%. [NBS household survey, 17 April] [NBS real estate, 17 April]

My view

The household survey and property data sharpen the composition problem. Income was rising, but consumption grew more slowly and the urban spending gain lagged the rural one, while new housing starts fell sharply. This suggests caution and a weak property channel rather than an absence of income growth. A stronger recovery would require households to spend more of that income and housing activity to stop worsening.

What to watch

The first-round shock now needs a second-round test

US real spending

March retail sales jumped in nominal terms as gasoline prices rose. Real categories and card volumes will show how much was demand and how much was price.

UK non-fuel demand

Fuel stockpiling lifted March retail volumes. Non-fuel retail and consumer services will show what remained after that timing effect.

China household spending

Real income rose faster than consumption while housing weakened. A higher share of income spent would signal less household caution.

Market and credit

Equities reversed the March fall quickly. Stable credit spreads and company margins would provide stronger confirmation that the disruption is contained.
