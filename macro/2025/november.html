Monthly Macro Highlights

November 2025 Macro Highlights

Businesses had started to change their plans rather than simply wait for clearer data. US banks treated large and small borrowers differently, UK companies reduced investment, and Chinese exporters sold more to markets outside the United States.

November 2025Reporting window: 28 October–24 November 2025By Jiefu Zhang

Central question

Were businesses only waiting for clearer data, or had they already changed their plans?

They had started to change them, although not every firm pulled back. Large US companies received better loan terms than small and trade-exposed firms. UK business investment fell even though services still grew. Chinese exporters sold more to ASEAN and Africa to offset weaker US sales, while property investment fell again.

What changed this month

Business plans gave a clearer signal than current sales

US loan terms diverged

Banks narrowed spreads and reduced some costs for large firms while tightening maximum credit lines and collateral terms for small firms.

Provisional UK business investment: −0.3% q/q

Capital expenditure fell in the third quarter even though it remained 0.7% above a year earlier.

China Jan–Oct exports to ASEAN: +14.3%

Shipments rose to ASEAN and Africa while exports to the United States fell 17.8%.

US high-yield spread: +33bp

The option-adjusted spread widened from 2.82% on 28 October to 3.15% on 24 November.

Evidence and mechanism

Loans and investment plans showed who was becoming more cautious

The Federal Reserve's October lending survey gave a more precise reading than the headline that banks had tightened commercial and industrial standards for firms of all sizes. Demand strengthened among large and mid-sized firms but was unchanged among small firms. Some terms for large borrowers improved, including larger credit lines, narrower spreads and lower costs. Small firms instead faced tighter maximum line sizes, collateral requirements and interest-rate floors. Banks also reported being more likely to approve firms with low exposure to trade disruption and less likely to approve highly exposed borrowers. [Federal Reserve lending survey, 3 November]

The reasons for borrowing mattered. Banks cited inventory accumulation and advance purchases related to trade developments as important sources of loan demand. Credit growth used to bring forward imports does not have the same implication as borrowing to expand capacity. One protects near-term supply; the other expresses confidence in future sales. The survey suggested both activity and caution could appear in the same loan aggregate.

Outside the United States, actual capital decisions had weakened. UK business investment fell 0.3% in the third quarter, while China's property investment fell 14.7% in the first ten months. Chinese exporters did make a new commitment of another kind: shipments to ASEAN and Africa expanded enough to keep total exports growing despite a steep fall in US-bound trade. That was commercial adaptation, but it shifted exposure towards the demand, logistics and trade rules of different destinations.

Answer

Companies were already changing their decisions. Investment had not stopped everywhere, but larger borrowers found it easier to obtain funding than smaller or trade-exposed firms. UK companies reduced capital spending, Chinese property investment weakened further, and exporters looked for new customers. These choices were likely to affect later orders and hiring even though GDP had not yet fallen.

Equities fell, high-yield spreads widened and volatility rose, which was a clear change from October's calm close. Funding was not freezing: the high-yield spread remained far below crisis levels and oil was almost unchanged. Investors were simply asking for more compensation to hold risky debt at the same time that banks were becoming more selective.

United States

Different terms by borrower

Banks offered better terms to large firms than to small ones

The delayed September employment report showed payrolls rising by 119,000 and unemployment at 4.4%. July and August gains were revised down by a combined 33,000. The data filled only part of October's visibility gap because no October household survey had been collected during the shutdown. More importantly for November's question, the lending survey showed decisions being made before a complete labour baseline returned. [BLS, 20 November]

Credit conditions divided by firm size and business model. Large companies reported stronger demand and received easier terms on some dimensions; small-company demand was unchanged while banks tightened line size and collateral. Approval appetite also depended on trade exposure. In household credit, mortgage demand strengthened but auto-loan demand weakened, and lenders were more willing to ease prime credit-card standards than near-prime or subprime standards.

My view

There was no single borrowing cost for the whole US economy. The terms depended on the size of the borrower, its collateral and how the bank viewed its risk. The 119,000 payroll gain did not remove that difference. Small-business credit lines and car finance were worth watching because weaker access there could affect orders and hiring before it appeared in total lending or GDP.

United Kingdom

Business investment fell

The investment fall was broader than one factory disruption

GDP rose 0.1% in the third quarter but fell 0.1% in September. Motor-vehicle manufacturing dropped 28.6% in the month and was the largest production drag, while services grew 0.2%. It was reasonable to treat part of the monthly output fall as a sector event. Business investment, however, fell 0.3% from the previous quarter. [ONS GDP, 13 November] [ONS business investment, 13 November]

The investment estimate was provisional and based on incomplete survey responses; large capital projects can also make quarterly data volatile. It nevertheless answered a different question from monthly production. A lost month of vehicle output reduces current GDP. A deferred machine, building or software project reduces future capacity and often the orders of domestic suppliers. The two may share a cause, but one cannot be used to dismiss the other.

The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 4% by a 5–4 vote, with four members preferring a quarter-point cut. That vote exposed disagreement about current risks; it did not guarantee the next decision. Even if the policy rate later fell, firms would still judge projects using expected sales, funding spreads and uncertainty, so capex need not respond immediately. [Bank of England, 6 November]

My view

Services kept the economy from contracting, but weaker investment suggested that the problem was not limited to one car-factory shutdown. A rebound in vehicle production would therefore tell us only part of the story. I would be less concerned if business investment were revised higher and new orders improved in both manufacturing and services.

China

Exports moved to new markets

New markets offset weaker exports to the United States

Goods exports rose 5.3% in January–October while imports fell 0.9%. The destination data explained how: exports to the United States fell 17.8%, while shipments to ASEAN rose 14.3% and those to Africa rose 26.1%. Growth in these alternative markets was large enough to keep total exports rising. [China Customs, 8 November]

This was evidence of exporter adaptation, not proof that final global demand had strengthened by the same amount. ASEAN-bound goods may serve local consumers, regional assembly chains or later re-export. Each route carries different margins and policy exposure. Imports falling while exports grew also meant external sales were not being matched by a comparable increase in purchases from abroad.

Meanwhile, real-estate development investment fell 14.7% in the first ten months. That was not November's lead topic—it continued October's capital-allocation divide—but it placed a limit on the domestic offset available if export routes became less profitable. [NBS real estate, 17 November]

My view

Selling more to ASEAN and Africa helped offset weaker US demand, but the headline export total did not show whether those sales were equally profitable. I would follow export prices, shipping routes and how quickly customers paid. Volumes could keep rising while lower prices or slower payment reduced the benefit to exporters.

Underfollowed indicator

A smaller credit line can matter even when the interest rate changes little

A small firm may continue to report that credit is available while losing the ability to draw enough cash for inventory, payroll or a new contract. November's survey found tighter maximum line sizes and collateral requirements for small businesses even as some pricing terms improved for large firms. That non-price rationing can change real decisions without a dramatic move in the average lending rate. It also explains why a policy-rate cut, if it comes, need not benefit every borrower at the same speed.

Since last month

November showed that firms had started to act

What continued

October's price sensitivity appeared again as banks and companies changed their plans.

What changed

Firm size, trade exposure and export destination increasingly affected access to credit and customers.

Still unclear

It was too early to know whether lower investment and borrowing would reduce current spending and output.

What would change my view

Stronger loan demand, easier terms for small firms and a recovery in UK investment would make the caution look temporary.
