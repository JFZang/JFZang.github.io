Monthly Macro Highlights

Growth continues,
but the balance is deteriorating

US inflation has firmed, UK output is close to flat, and China’s third-quarter slowdown leaves manufacturing ahead of domestic demand.

October 2025Reporting window: 1–27 October 2025By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

The expansion is intact, but its quality is less reassuring. Price pressure has returned in parts of the US, Britain has little momentum, and China is growing through industry more than construction or consumption.

Global

The headline cycle is becoming less useful

There is no single global direction. The US data blackout has made the strongest major economy harder to read, the UK is close to a standstill, and China’s 4.8% third-quarter growth still rests disproportionately on manufacturing. [NBS, Q3 GDP] Country and sector selection should matter more than a broad call on the cycle.

Markets are also receiving conflicting signals. Higher US gasoline prices show that the inflation shock has not disappeared, while demand-sensitive parts of the UK and Chinese economies are losing speed. That combination can support defensive assets and long-duration growth companies at the same time; it does not fit neatly into a conventional risk-on or risk-off label.

My view

I still favour a slowdown over a recession, mainly because none of the three economies is showing the broad fall in jobs, output and orders that normally marks a contraction. The risk is that weak demand and renewed fuel inflation arrive together: households would lose purchasing power just as central banks became less able to help. That makes me selective rather than broadly bearish. Businesses with repeat purchases and manageable debt have more control over earnings than those waiting for a strong recovery. A simultaneous improvement in hiring and new orders, without another rise in core inflation, would make me less cautious.

Policy and market implications

The information gap deserves a risk premium

September US CPI was released late in the month, but the shutdown has interrupted the normal sequence of labour and activity reports. That matters because confidence in the path of Federal Reserve policy depends on data that are temporarily missing. Meanwhile, the 4.1% monthly rise in gasoline has made energy an immediate household issue rather than an abstract commodity move. [BLS, 24 October]

Any unusually large move in bond yields, the dollar or volatility should be read with caution while the information set is incomplete. Price action can reveal positioning and fear, but it cannot replace the absent economic evidence.

United States

Inflation firms

Gasoline complicates the picture

September CPI rose 0.3% on the month and 3.0% over the year. Gasoline increased 4.1% and was the largest contributor to the monthly rise. [BLS, 24 October]

My view

Energy-driven inflation should not be treated like persistent services pressure because fuel prices can reverse more quickly than wages or rents. It still reduces household purchasing power, however, and can weaken spending before the headline rate falls again. With employment data delayed by the shutdown, the Fed has less visibility than usual precisely when the trade-off is becoming harder.

United Kingdom

Near standstill

August offers little reassurance

Monthly GDP grew 0.1% in August, while the three-month expansion remained modest. Production improved, but underlying activity was subdued. [ONS, 16 October]

My view

The economy is avoiding contraction without building much momentum. When growth is this weak, even a small rise in mortgage costs or fall in confidence can materially change household spending. Domestically focused companies therefore have less room to absorb disappointing demand than the positive GDP headline suggests.

China

Slower, still industrial

Manufacturing outpaces the economy

Third-quarter GDP grew 4.8% year on year, down from 5.2% in the second quarter. Manufacturing expanded 6.3%, while construction contracted 2.3% and real estate output fell 0.2%. [NBS, Q3 GDP]

My view

The important October development is not simply that manufacturing is stronger than consumption; it is that the gap has survived another quarter while construction has contracted. Factory-led growth can support exporters and their suppliers, but it gives households less support than a recovery in property and services because it does not repair housing wealth or confidence. I therefore see a better backdrop for firms selling competitive products abroad than for businesses relying on a broad domestic spending rebound. A turn in housing transactions and construction would challenge that distinction.

What to watch

Where the imbalance shows next

US data gap

The longer the shutdown obscures labour conditions, the more policy expectations will depend on noisy substitutes. A clean resumption of payroll data would reduce that uncertainty.

Energy prices

Gasoline has already lifted September CPI. Persistence matters because several months of higher fuel bills would weaken consumption and raise transport costs.

UK demand

Monthly GDP is barely positive. A convincing recovery would require services volumes and consumer-facing activity to improve together.

China property

Construction and real-estate output are contracting even as manufacturing expands. Further weakness would pressure households, local finances and demand for materials.
