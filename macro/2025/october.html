Monthly Macro Highlights

A US data gap obscured the labour market, UK spending shifted towards goods, and China’s industrial investment rose while private and property investment fell.

October 2025Reporting window: 1–27 October 2025By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

October does not show one global cycle. It shows three different constraints: missing US labour data, a UK split between goods spending and consumer services, and Chinese growth supported by industrial investment rather than property.

Global

Data gaps obscure a modest slowdown

The first problem this month is visibility. Scheduled US labour releases were missing, UK consumer-facing services contracted over the latest three months, and China’s 4.8% third-quarter growth relied heavily on manufacturing. [BLS release calendar] [ONS, 16 October] [NBS, Q3 GDP]

These are not three versions of the same weakness. The US signal is incomplete, UK households bought more goods even as consumer services fell, and China’s constraint sits in property and private investment. Treating all three as a common demand slowdown would hide more than it explains.

My view

I read the evidence as a slowdown without a synchronised contraction. None of the three economies yet shows a broad fall in jobs, output and orders. The harder case would be weak demand arriving with persistent fuel inflation: households would lose purchasing power just as central banks became less able to support activity.

Policy and market implications

Equities rise despite a thinner US data set

The Federal Reserve had cut its target range by 25 basis points in September as downside risks to employment increased. The Bank of England kept Bank Rate at 4% while UK inflation remained well above target. [Federal Reserve, 17 September] [Bank of England, 18 September]

Markets did not treat the US data gap as evidence of recession. The S&P 500 rose from 6,711.20 on 1 October to 6,875.16 on 27 October, a gain of about 2.4% during the reporting window. [FRED, S&P 500] That move is useful evidence about risk appetite, but it does not replace the missing labour data; prices can rise because investors expect future earnings or easier policy even while current activity is unclear.

My view

I think the equity response is understandable but not informative enough to settle the macro question. A data gap should reduce confidence in both bearish and bullish claims. The next labour release matters more than the index move because it will show whether the Fed cut into a mild slowdown or something broader.

United States

Spending softens

Fuel lifts CPI while household spending softens

September CPI rose 0.3% on the month and 3.0% over the year. Gasoline increased 4.1% and made the largest contribution to the monthly rise, while core CPI slowed to 0.2%. [BLS, 24 October]

The Federal Reserve’s October Beige Book said activity changed little and retail spending edged down. Auto sales received a temporary lift before an electric-vehicle tax credit expired, while lower- and middle-income consumers looked for discounts. [Federal Reserve Beige Book, 15 October]

My view

The gasoline increase is a cost shock, not evidence that underlying demand strengthened. The spending evidence is also mixed: discount seeking points to pressure on household budgets, but the expiring tax credit distorted auto sales. I would put more weight on whether weaker retail demand spreads beyond that timing effect than on one headline CPI print.

United Kingdom

Goods beat services

Goods spending improves while consumer services weaken

Monthly GDP grew 0.1% in August and 0.3% over the latest three months. Services grew 0.4% over that period, but consumer-facing services fell 0.6%. [ONS, 16 October]

Retail volumes moved the other way. They rose 0.5% in September and 0.9% in the third quarter, helped by non-store retail and computer and telecom sales. [ONS retail sales, 24 October] CPI remained 3.8% year on year and services inflation was 4.7%. [ONS, 22 October]

My view

Household demand is changing composition rather than moving in one direction. Goods volumes improved while consumer-facing services contracted, so the aggregate GDP figure alone is too blunt. The problem for the Bank of England is that services inflation remains high even where service volumes are weak; that limits how quickly policy can respond to softer activity.

China

Capital shifts to industry

Industrial investment rises while property contracts

Third-quarter GDP grew 4.8% year on year. Manufacturing expanded 6.3%, while construction contracted 2.3% and real-estate output fell 0.2%. September industrial value added grew 6.5%. [NBS, Q3 GDP] [NBS industrial production]

The split is clearer in investment. Fixed-asset investment fell 0.5% in the first nine months and private investment declined 3.1%, while industrial investment rose 6.4%. Real-estate development investment fell 13.9%, new starts 18.9% and property sales by value 7.9%. [NBS fixed-asset investment] [NBS real estate]

My view

The main October divide is where capital is going. Industrial investment can keep factory output growing, but falling private and property investment does little to repair household balance sheets or confidence. I would change that view if housing sales, private investment and retail growth began to improve together; one more strong production figure would not be enough.

What to watch

What would separate the three constraints

US data return

The first complete labour releases will show whether the shutdown hid a broad employment slowdown or only delayed confirmation of gradual cooling.

UK spending mix

Retail volumes improved while consumer-facing services fell. The next releases should show whether households keep shifting between goods and services or reduce both.

China capital allocation

Private, industrial and property investment are moving in different directions. A turn in private and housing investment would matter more than another factory-output gain.

Market confirmation

Equities rose through the data gap. Credit spreads and company guidance will show whether that confidence extends beyond large listed firms.
