October 2025 Macro Highlights

The US shutdown delayed several important releases. To judge whether the weakness was broad, I looked instead at how firms set prices, what UK households bought and where Chinese investment was going.

October 2025Reporting window: 1–27 October 2025By Jiefu Zhang

Central question

When important US data were delayed, how could we tell whether the weakness was broad?

The remaining data did not show activity falling everywhere. Some US firms could raise prices while others accepted lower profits, UK households spent more on goods but less on consumer services, and Chinese investment moved away from property and private projects towards industry. These details could not replace the missing jobs report, but they could show where demand was already weak.

What changed this month

With key US releases delayed, the remaining details mattered more

US labour visibility fell

The shutdown delayed the September employment report. September CPI was released on 24 October because collection had finished before the funding lapse.

Pricing power split

The Federal Reserve's Beige Book found both full tariff pass-through and firms holding prices to protect market share.

UK retail volumes: +0.5% m/m

September sales rose even though consumer-facing services had fallen 0.6% over the three months to August.

China Jan–Sep private investment: −3.1%

Private fixed investment fell while industrial investment rose 6.4%.

Evidence and mechanism

The available data could still answer a narrower question

No private survey or market price could fully replace the missing payroll report. The September CPI release still gave some information: the index rose 0.3% in the month and 3.0% over the year, with gasoline's 4.1% increase making the largest contribution. Core CPI rose 0.2%, while owners' equivalent rent increased only 0.1%, its smallest monthly move since January 2021. The increase therefore looked more connected to fuel than to a general rise in demand. [BLS, 24 October]

The October Beige Book added detail that the CPI total could not show. Input costs rose across Federal Reserve districts, often because of tariffs, but firms reacted differently. Some raised prices in full. Others delayed the increase or accepted lower profits because their customers were sensitive to price. Retail activity fell in several districts, and lower- and middle-income households looked for discounts more often. The survey could not measure national output, but it showed how weaker demand could reduce company profits before appearing as fewer transactions in official data. [Federal Reserve Beige Book, 15 October]

The UK and China also did not fit one common explanation. UK retail volumes increased, helped by clothing, online sales and computer and telecom products, while consumer-facing services fell over three months. In China, manufacturing and equipment investment grew while private, property and service investment fell. Total growth alone did not show these changes in where money was being spent.

Answer

The data available by 27 October did not show weakness everywhere. Consumers were changing what they bought, firms were choosing between higher prices and lower margins, and Chinese investment was still growing in selected industries. I therefore did not have enough evidence to call a broad contraction. That would need to appear later in jobs, orders, credit and spending at the same time.

The changes during the month mattered more than the calm closing level. Investors reacted to individual risks and then reversed much of the move. By the end of the window, the shutdown and weaker business reports had not caused a lasting increase in financing costs. This still told us nothing about whether the missing labour data would be strong.

United States

Firms absorbed costs differently

Firms' pricing decisions gave a clue while payroll data were missing

The Beige Book described activity as little changed overall, with retail spending edging down and several districts reporting weaker sales. Auto purchases received a temporary lift before an electric-vehicle tax credit expired, so that category could not be read as durable demand. At the same time, gasoline lifted headline CPI while core and shelter inflation slowed. The available pieces therefore separated an input-cost rise from a household spending acceleration.

Company behaviour gave the clearest short-term clue. A business that raises prices without losing sales can make customers pay the higher cost. A business that keeps prices unchanged, offers discounts or accepts lower profits is showing that customers may leave if prices rise. The Beige Book is not an official index, so it cannot estimate GDP. It can still show where demand is already limiting price increases.

My view

US demand looked slow, but the evidence was not strong enough to call a contraction. I would worry more if the delayed employment report showed job losses together with weaker retail sales, orders and credit. If employment stayed positive and mainly price-sensitive businesses absorbed higher costs, the problem would look more like pressure on margins than an economy-wide decline.

United Kingdom

Goods up, services down

Retail improved, but consumer services were still weak

September retail volumes rose 0.5% from August, 0.9% over the quarter and 1.5% from a year earlier. Online retail recorded an eighth consecutive monthly increase, computer and telecom sales were strong, and the online share reached 28.0%. Yet total volumes remained 1.6% below February 2020. The August GDP release added the service detail: total services grew 0.4% over three months, but consumer-facing services fell 0.6%. [ONS retail sales, 24 October] [ONS GDP, 16 October]

September CPI was 3.8% and services inflation 4.7%. That did not prove strong service demand: prices can remain high because wages, rents or regulated costs adjust slowly even when volumes weaken. The simultaneous move from services towards goods and online channels also changes which businesses receive household cash, making a single consumption label especially unhelpful.

My view

UK households were still spending, but the improvement was concentrated in a few types of goods and in online sales. One stronger retail month was not enough to call a rebound while consumer services remained weak and total volumes were still below 2020. I wanted to see both discretionary services and goods improve in later releases.

China

Property investment weakened

Equipment investment rose while private and property investment fell

Third-quarter GDP grew 4.8% from a year earlier. Manufacturing expanded 6.3%, construction contracted 2.3% and real-estate output fell 0.2%. The investment detail was sharper: fixed-asset investment fell 0.5% in the first nine months, private investment declined 3.1% and foreign-invested enterprise spending fell 12.6%. Industrial investment nevertheless rose 6.4%, manufacturing investment 4.0% and equipment purchases 14.0%. [NBS, Q3 GDP] [NBS, fixed-asset investment]

Property remained the main weak area. Development investment fell 13.9%, new starts 18.9% and sales by value 7.9% in January–September. Industrial investment could support output and machinery orders, but it did not give developers more cash or improve household wealth tied to housing. I therefore wanted to know whether private firms were willing to invest outside the industrial areas receiving the most support.

My view

The 14% rise in equipment purchases showed that upgrading was continuing in supported areas. It did not mean that firms generally wanted to invest more. I would read the picture differently if private investment, property sales and service investment also began to rise. Until then, the data mainly showed where investment was being redirected.

Underfollowed indicator

Whether firms raise prices tells us something about demand

Input-cost indices tell us what businesses pay; consumer-price indices tell us what customers eventually pay. The step between them is the commercial decision to raise a list price, add a surcharge, reduce a discount or accept a smaller margin. October's Beige Book captured that middle step. The most useful distinction was not “tariffs raised costs,” which was already known. It was that businesses facing less price-sensitive customers passed costs through while others protected volume. Tracking this split alongside margins and unit sales can reveal weak demand before it becomes an obvious fall in aggregate spending.

Series starting point

October left several questions for the next few months

What we knew

Costs were rising and some customers were cutting back, but the weakness was not economy-wide.

Still unclear

Without the US labour releases, I could not tell whether weaker customer demand had reached hiring.

What to watch next

If firms expected the weakness to last, they would probably change credit lines, investment and future orders.

What would change my view

Employment, orders, credit and spending falling together would support a broader contraction call.
