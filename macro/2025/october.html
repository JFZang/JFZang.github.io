Monthly Macro Highlights

US headline inflation edged higher on energy, UK growth remained weak while inflation stayed elevated, and China’s industrial strength continued to outpace property and household demand.

October 2025Reporting window: 1–27 October 2025By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

The expansion is intact, but its quality is less reassuring. Energy lifted US headline inflation even as core inflation slowed, Britain combined weak momentum with above-target inflation, and China continued to grow faster through manufacturing than through property or retail demand. These are different problems, but each limits how much support policymakers can provide if growth weakens.

Global

The headline cycle is becoming less useful

There is no single global direction. The absence of scheduled US labour releases made the strongest major economy harder to read, UK consumer-facing services contracted over the latest three months, and China’s 4.8% third-quarter growth still rested disproportionately on manufacturing. [BLS, October release calendar] [ONS, 16 October] [NBS, Q3 GDP]

The policy settings also differed. The Federal Reserve had cut its target range by 25 basis points in September as downside risks to employment increased, while the Bank of England kept Bank Rate at 4% because UK inflation remained well above target. [Federal Reserve, 17 September] [Bank of England, 18 September] China faced a different constraint: industrial production was expanding, but weak property investment and slower retail growth reduced the domestic transmission of that strength.

My view

I still favour a slowdown over a recession, mainly because none of the three economies is showing the broad fall in jobs, output and orders that normally marks a contraction. The risk is that weak demand and another sustained rise in fuel costs arrive together: households would lose purchasing power just as central banks became less able to help. That makes me selective rather than broadly bearish. Businesses with repeat purchases and manageable debt should have more control over cash flow than highly leveraged firms waiting for a strong recovery. I would turn more bearish if the delayed labour data eventually showed broad job losses while new-order surveys remained in contraction. If core inflation also stayed elevated, the slowdown would look increasingly stagflationary because the Fed would have little room to support demand.

Policy and market implications

The information gap deserves a risk premium

September US CPI was released late in the month, but the lapse in government services interrupted the normal sequence of labour and activity reports. [BLS, October release calendar] That matters because the Fed had just cut rates in response to a changed balance of risks, yet the next scheduled employment evidence was unavailable. Gasoline then rose 4.1% in September and was the largest contributor to the monthly CPI increase, although it was still 0.5% cheaper than a year earlier. [BLS, 24 October]

The UK presented the opposite information problem: the data were available, but they pointed in conflicting directions. Weak activity argued for support, while 3.8% CPI inflation and 4.7% services inflation argued for patience. [ONS, 22 October] Across both countries, unusually large moves in bond yields or currencies should therefore be read with care. Market prices can reveal positioning and expectations, but neither a US data gap nor a UK growth-inflation conflict offers a clean policy signal.

United States

Headline inflation edges up

Gasoline complicates an otherwise softer month

September CPI rose 0.3% on the month and 3.0% over the year, up from 2.9% in August. Gasoline increased 4.1% and was the largest contributor to the monthly rise. Core CPI, however, slowed to 0.2% on the month from 0.3% in each of the previous two months. [BLS, 24 October]

My view

One month of energy-driven headline inflation should not be treated like persistent services pressure: fuel prices can reverse more quickly than wages or rents, and gasoline remained lower than a year earlier. It still reduces household purchasing power while it lasts. With scheduled employment data absent, the Fed had less visibility than usual precisely when the balance between inflation and demand was becoming harder to judge. Several further months of firm core inflation—not this gasoline move alone—would make the inflation concern more durable.

United Kingdom

Weak beneath the headline

Three-month growth masks soft household demand

Monthly GDP grew 0.1% in August, and output increased 0.3% in the three months to August. Services grew 0.4% over that three-month period, but consumer-facing services fell 0.6%; in August alone, services were flat while production rose 0.4%. [ONS, 16 October]

Inflation left little room for complacency. September CPI was unchanged on the month but remained 3.8% higher than a year earlier. Core CPI eased slightly to 3.5%, while services inflation stayed at 4.7%. The Bank of England had maintained Bank Rate at 4% in September, with two members preferring a quarter-point cut. [ONS, 22 October] [Bank of England, 18 September]

My view

The economy is avoiding contraction without generating a broad improvement in household demand. The decline in consumer-facing services matters more for domestically focused companies than the positive aggregate GDP figure. At the same time, persistent services inflation limits the Bank’s ability to offset weak demand. A sustained rise in consumer-facing activity alongside lower services inflation—not another isolated positive GDP month—would make the outlook more convincing.

China

Slower, still industrial

Manufacturing outpaces property and retail demand

Third-quarter GDP grew 4.8% year on year, down from 5.2% in the second quarter. Manufacturing expanded 6.3%, while construction contracted 2.3% and real estate output fell 0.2%. September industrial value added grew 6.5% year on year, including 7.3% growth in manufacturing. [NBS, Q3 GDP] [NBS, September industrial production]

The domestic side was weaker. September retail sales grew 3.0% year on year, down from 3.4% in August. Fixed-asset investment fell 0.5% over the first nine months and private investment declined 3.1%, even as industrial investment increased 6.4%. Real-estate development investment dropped 13.9%, new starts fell 18.9%, and sales by value declined 7.9%. [NBS, September retail sales] [NBS, fixed-asset investment] [NBS, real-estate development]

My view

The important October development is that manufacturing’s lead over property and retail demand survived another quarter. The contrast between rising industrial investment and falling private and property investment shows that the imbalance extends beyond one GDP table. Factory-led growth can support exporters and suppliers, but it does less to repair housing wealth or household confidence. I therefore see a better backdrop for firms selling competitive products abroad than for businesses relying on a broad domestic spending rebound. A sustained turn in housing sales, private investment and retail growth would challenge that distinction.

What to watch

Where the imbalance shows next

US data gap

The longer scheduled labour releases remain absent, the more policy expectations will depend on noisy substitutes. A clean resumption of payroll data would reduce that uncertainty.

Energy prices

Gasoline lifted September CPI but remained cheaper than a year earlier. Persistence, rather than one monthly jump, will determine whether fuel weakens consumption and raises transport costs.

UK demand

Consumer-facing services fell over the latest three months. A convincing recovery would require this measure and broader services volumes to improve together.

China property and retail

Construction and real-estate output contracted while September retail growth slowed. Further weakness would pressure households, local finances and domestic demand.
