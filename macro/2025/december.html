Monthly Macro Highlights

The year ends
with momentum thinning

US hiring has slowed, Britain is contracting at the margin, and China’s consumer recovery remains narrower than its industrial base.

December 2025Reporting window: 25 November–19 December 2025By Jiefu Zhang

The month in one view

Inflation is becoming less severe, but demand is not taking over cleanly. The final releases of the year suggest that 2026 begins with less pricing pressure and less economic momentum.

Global

Relief arrives with a weaker pulse

The immediate policy trade-off is improving as inflation cools, yet softer labour and activity data make the improvement less celebratory than it first appears. US unemployment is 4.6%, UK output has fallen for two consecutive months, and Chinese retail growth is only 1.3%. [BLS, 16 December] [ONS, 12 December] [NBS, 16 December] The data point in the same direction even though the causes differ.

Policy is starting to respond. Both the Federal Reserve and Bank of England have reduced rates in December, but neither decision implies an aggressive easing cycle. The important market question is whether lower discount rates arrive quickly enough to offset weaker cash-flow expectations.

My view

I would enter the new year cautiously optimistic. Lower inflation helps households because pay goes further, while lower policy rates gradually reduce the cost of new borrowing. Neither effect guarantees a strong earnings recovery: December’s weaker employment and spending data suggest that relief is arriving because demand has softened. I would add more cyclical exposure only after hiring stabilises and new orders improve across more than one major economy. Otherwise, rate cuts may support valuations before they support company revenues.

Policy and market implications

Rate cuts do not settle the argument

The Federal Reserve lowered its target range to 3.5–3.75% on 10 December. Eight days later, the Bank of England cut Bank Rate to 3.75% by a narrow 5–4 vote; the dissenters preferred no change. [Federal Reserve, 10 December] [Bank of England, 18 December]

The split votes and cautious guidance matter more than the shared 3.75% upper rate. [Federal Reserve, 10 December] [Bank of England, 18 December] Markets can reasonably expect less restraint in 2026, but not a return to cheap money. Credit quality and refinancing schedules therefore remain part of the macro story.

United States

Labour softens

The missing data return with a warning

November payrolls rose by 64,000 and unemployment was 4.6%. Health care and construction added jobs, while federal employment continued to fall. [BLS, 16 December]

My view

The labour market is not collapsing, but it has clearly lost speed. Businesses usually cut vacancies and slow recruitment before they begin widespread layoffs, so weaker hiring can be an early sign that household income growth will cool. The data disruption argues for humility; even so, the direction gives the Fed more reason to protect employment.

United Kingdom

Stalling

Inflation falls as output slips

GDP fell 0.1% in October and over the latest three months. Services declined 0.3% in the month, while production rose 1.1%. [ONS, 12 December]

November CPI inflation eased to 3.2% from 3.6%; services inflation slowed to 4.4%. [ONS, 17 December]

My view

The inflation news creates room for eventual relief, but falling services output is the more urgent signal. Lower rates can help property and other borrowing-dependent businesses first because their financing costs respond directly. A broader consumer recovery still needs wages to rise faster than prices and households to feel secure enough to spend.

China

Demand lags

Consumption loses pace

November retail sales grew 1.3% year on year, while online physical-goods sales were 5.7% higher over the first eleven months. [NBS, 16 December]

My view

The slowdown in retail demand makes a growth model led by factories harder to sustain without stronger exports. If households do not buy the additional output, companies must sell more abroad or accept weaker prices and margins. I therefore remain more constructive on competitive manufacturers than on businesses waiting for a broad household-spending revival.

What to watch

The first signals of 2026

US unemployment

At 4.6%, unemployment has moved beyond a cosmetic rise. [BLS, 16 December] Claims and hiring breadth will distinguish slower recruitment from outright job loss.

UK services

Services are the main stabiliser, so another decline would make the downturn more general. Business volumes matter more than confidence alone.

China retail

Holiday spending will test whether households can recover after November’s 1.3% growth. [NBS, 16 December] A recovery would reduce reliance on exports and factory investment.

Rate transmission

Policy rates have fallen, but households and firms borrow at market rates. Credit volumes and refinancing spreads will show whether relief reaches them.
